# Start 04 — Making the Case at Your Agency — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — Making the Case at Your Agency

This is Episode 4 of the Start series, the last one, from the Federal Modernization Workbook. The first three episodes made the case and walked through the first eight weeks. This one is about the meeting where you ask for the go-ahead. It turns the case into one page: four local numbers, a pitch shaped for each person at the table, one decision with an owner and a date, and the roles that sign. The framing and the numbers are design guidance, so adapt them freely. Let's start with the page.

## Slide 2 — One page, one decision

The one-page ask has three parts. A short reminder of why now: deadlines set outside the agency, and identity as the thing everything else depends on. Then your own numbers, which make the case local. Then one decision: approve the four-week pre-flight, with a named owner and a start date. It's deliberately small. It needs no vendor choice, just an inventory, an owner and a date. Everything after that is decided at gates, from evidence. So, which numbers?

## Slide 3 — Four local numbers that make it real

Four numbers do more than any slide. How many Windows Server 2016 servers you run, since support ends in January 2027 and many domain controllers run on it. How many contractors sit outside the HR system, because HR 2.0 covers federal employees only. How long a departing person keeps access, from the day HR records the separation to the last account disabled. And your Federal HR 2.0 wave and dates, because the chance to design identity in closes when your HR connections go live. Each person at the table hears these differently.

## Slide 4 — For the CIO: the clocks and the cost

For the CIO, lead with the clocks, and the cost of meeting them piecemeal. Server end-of-support dates, the edge-device directive and the new logging plan all land on the same estate. A set of separate projects can only race those dates; one program can sequence them. The first step costs four weeks and no vendor decision. Be straight about later costs: the program has no license of its own, but the cloud services it uses do. And running past end of support becomes a risk someone must formally accept. Next, the CISO.

## Slide 5 — For the CISO: leavers and account findings

For the CISO, lead with leavers. When HR records a separation, nothing else is listening. A ticket disables the main account days later, other accounts stay live, and a review months on finds a few of them. That's not carelessness; it's the wiring. It's also where account-management findings come from, since the controls expect accounts to follow termination and transfer. HR-driven identity fixes the cause, starting with leavers, and measures the time from separation to block from day one. That number is your before and after.

## Slide 6 — For the CHCO: HR 2.0 and the contractor source

For the chief human capital officer, lead with timing. HR 2.0 moves the agency onto one government-wide platform whether or not identity rides along. During onboarding, the agency designs its new connections, and that's the moment to add the identity feed. Once those connections go live, adding it means reopening them. The second point is contractors. HR 2.0 covers federal employees, so contractors need a source of their own, and onboarding is the right time to choose it. HR decides what the attributes mean; identity decides how they flow.

## Slide 7 — For the authorizing official: evidence over attestation

For the authorizing official, lead with evidence. They're the only official who can accept risk for the agency, and every risk the program carries, rather than removes, reaches them in writing, with an end date. In return, they get something better than attestation. Every gate is checked from data, every change is a signed plan, and evidence comes out of the work instead of being assembled before an assessment. Automated changes stay gated, with a person approving before anything runs. That's a posture an authorizing official can sign.

## Slide 8 — The decision: an owner, a date and a first gate

Write the decision as one sentence someone can say yes to. Approve the four-week pre-flight, sponsored by a named executive, run by a named steward, starting on a set date, with the first gate on the calendar beside the external deadlines. Before saying yes, a sponsor should be able to answer a few questions. Is the source of truth under version control? Can every change be traced? Does every decision have one named decider? If the room hesitates on any of those, that's useful too. Next, who signs what.

## Slide 9 — Charter roles: who signs what

The charter fixes the shape and leaves the names blank for you. The executive sponsor, with the CIO, owns scope, funding and sequence, and decides when directory components retire. The steward owns the target design and the repository, and clears the early gates. Workstream leads say when their area is ready. Around them, the CISO sets the control requirements, the authorizing official accepts risk, and HR owns what the workforce data means. Many of these people already hold the zero trust and identity governance roles federal policy required.

## Slide 10 — What to bring to the meeting

Bring the one-page ask, with your four numbers filled in. Bring the external deadlines that apply to your estate, candidate names for sponsor and steward, a proposed start date, and a date for the first gate. And leave the room with one decision recorded, whatever it is.
