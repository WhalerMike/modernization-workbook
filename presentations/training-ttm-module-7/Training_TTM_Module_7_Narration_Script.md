## Slide 2 — Why ticket history doesn't prove the month

Why does this matter? At the end of the month, your ISSO needs to show an assessor three things. That every identity finding was owned, clocked, re-read and closed. That nothing was edited after it closed. And that nothing was quietly dropped. Ticket history and screenshots don't prove that. A ticket shows a status, not a re-read. And neither one can show an edit that left no trace, or a record that's simply missing. In this module you learn what Gate 7 runs and reports, then build and verify the evidence package for a period, a package that can prove all three.

## Slide 4 — Gate 7: operating the tempo

Once the loop runs, it runs on the workbook's tempo. P1 halts. P2 has one hour, P3 twenty-four hours, and P4 seventy-two hours. Those are the program's windows, not federal ones. Gate 7 is how you operate it, in five steps. Schedule the drift reconciliation and the re-read client, and keep their outputs with the date and the checker version. Report five measures to the governance board. Route the identity fixes through the loop. Export the evidence in the shape of the workbook's evidence record. And review each declared exception at its end date, and each L4 class quarterly. The guide's Gate 7 validation names the export as its automated check, and Chapter 07 builds its ServiceNow half. That's what you'll run in the lab.

## Slide 5 — Five measures for the governance board

Gate 7 reports five measures to the governance board, from the workbook's design: detection coverage, window adherence, escalation rate, machine-closure share, and the declared-exception count. How is Gate 7 validated? The automated checks are read-only. The evidence export writes and checks the records, and the drift comparison from Module 4 classifies findings across two runs. The five measures themselves have no automated check yet. The manual validation is that the scheduled runs have run for a month with outputs kept, the measures have been reported once, and the first monthly package has been assembled end to end and accepted by the ISSO. Until the export exists in your estate, the interim path is to assemble the package by hand, from a ServiceNow report of closed findings with their evidence attachments and a SailPoint event search for the same period.

## Slide 6 — The workbook's evidence record, field by field

Every lane writes the same record, the workbook's evidence record, with seven fields. The guide shows where each comes from on these products. Origin is the SailPoint trigger and workflow, the reconciliation finding, or the catalog request. Owner and clock are the ServiceNow assignment, severity, priority and due date. Approval is the ServiceNow or SailPoint approval. Executing identity is the connector's or spoke's service principal, or a person's PIM activation. What ran is the provisioning event, the workflow version or the session recording. Result is the re-read evidence, and closure is the closed record with its hash. In Pass 1, three of those are empty: approval, executing identity and what ran. Pass 1 doesn't actuate, so the record that would hold them is a later pass. The export writes each as null, with that reason, rather than leaving the field out or filling it.

## Slide 7 — One record per finding, and four checks on each

The re-read client from Module 6 already writes the evidence onto each record it closes: the evidence itself as canonical JSON, its SHA-256 hash, the verification time, a close note and an attachment. But nothing read the records back out. Chapter 07 adds the evidence export. It reads closed and cancelled findings through the Table API, under its own read-only role, and writes one record per finding in the shape of the workbook's evidence record. Then it runs four checks on each, reported by name. Does the evidence still hash to the stored hash? If not, it was edited after closure, or cut short by the four-thousand-character field. Does a closed record carry evidence at all? If not, the close bypassed the business rule. Does the evidence say the intended state matched? And did it finish inside its response window, verified at or before its due date?

## Slide 8 — The package: three files, a chain, and a verifier

The export packages the period in three files. Records dot J S O N L holds one record per line, and each line carries the SHA-256 of the line before it, so a removed or reordered record breaks the chain. Summary dot JSON holds window adherence, the count of declared exceptions and the records with problems. And the manifest holds a SHA-256 for each file and the chain head. The previous-manifest option continues last period's chain into this one. The generated-at option fixes the timestamp, so the same records always give the same bytes. And verify-dir recomputes every hash and walks the chain. That's the check an ISSO runs on a package they receive. One caution: the package is tamper-evident, not signed. Anyone who can rewrite every file and the manifest can rewrite the chain. So, as design guidance, keep each manifest where the exporter can't write, and sign it if the program requires.

## Slide 9 — Window adherence, and five fictional records

Window adherence needs care, because it's easy to measure in a way that flatters. Chapter 07 measures it the way the workbook's design defines it: closures inside the window, over closures judged. It isn't a ratio of open findings, which improves as new ones arrive. The kit's fixture has five fictional closed records, one per case: one closed in its window, one closed late, one whose evidence was edited after it closed, one cancelled as a declared exception, and one closed with no evidence at all. You'll export all five in the lab. One thing wasn't verified: the format and time zone of the date values the Table API returns on a given instance. The export parses both common forms, and Lab 2 checks yours.

## Slide 10 — Dangers to the evidence, and the guard for each

Chapter 07 lists the dangers to the evidence, and the guard for each. A record edited after closure would say something the re-read didn't, so the export recomputes the evidence hash and reports a mismatch. Evidence cut short by the field length can't be parsed, so it's reported, and the attachment holds the full copy. A close that bypassed the business rule is named: closed without re-read evidence. A record deleted from the period would make the package look complete, but the chain breaks and the verifier reports it. Fields filled in to look complete would claim an approval that never happened, so later-pass fields stay null with their reason, and tests pin that. And if the export ran with the verifier's role, it could close findings, so it runs under its own read-only role, which the kit's call list can check.

## Slide 12 — Try it: chain November, then remove a record

Step four starts November's chain from October's head. The export runs over the same fixture for period November, with the previous-manifest option pointing at October's manifest. On the slide, its first five lines are trimmed, because they repeat step two. The last line reports November's chain head, and November's manifest records October's head as its chain previous. So a period can't be swapped out without breaking the next one. Step five is the test that matters to an ISSO. A one-line Python command deletes the second record from October's records file, and then you verify again. The verifier finds two problems: the file's SHA-256 no longer matches the manifest, and the next line's previous hash no longer matches the line before it. The package does not hold. Step six deletes the out folder.

## Slide 14 — The federal requirement, and the evidence to keep

The federal requirement. The guide's Gate 7 table maps the tempo to NIST special publication eight hundred fifty-three: continuous monitoring, risk response, the plan of action and milestones, and audit record review. Chapter 07 maps the records and the package to configuration change control, continuous monitoring, and A U nine, protection of audit information. It also says what isn't evidenced yet: no approval, executing identity or run log until actuation is built. The evidence an ISSO keeps: the scheduled run outputs and the five measures, window adherence and the overdue list, the exception register, and the monthly package and its review; and from Chapter 07, the records, the manifest and the chain, verified, and the summary file. Two controls the workbook maps to the loop, A U ten and C M five enhancement one, aren't in NIST's Moderate baseline. At Moderate, the chain supports integrity, and signing the package is a further step.
