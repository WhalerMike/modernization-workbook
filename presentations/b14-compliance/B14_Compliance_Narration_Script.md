# Book 14 — Federal Compliance & Authorization — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — Can Your Authorization Prove Itself?

Welcome. This briefing comes from Book 14 of the Federal Modernization Workbook, Federal Compliance and Authorization. It's written for authorizing officials, information system security officers and CIOs. I'll take four questions. Why does every control need proof rather than a promise, and what is the federal clock asking for now? What do the baseline, the closure rule and the agency-level control families require? Who owns compliance? And how do you build the authorization package and keep it current? If you remember one thing, make it this: a control is closed when the thing that closes it is running, and the record shows it working. Everything else is a promise, and promises age. Let's start with who owes which proof.

## Slide 2 — Who owes which proof

Compliance usually gets described as a long list of obligations. What an authorizing official actually needs is narrower: to know each control is working today, and to show anyone who asks how they know. That's the difference between a promise and proof. A promise is a line in the security plan, backed by evidence someone gathered by hand for the last assessment. It was true the day it was signed, and it's been aging ever since. Proof is a record the system produces itself, one another reviewer can check and get the same answer. So who owes what? The cloud provider proves its service under FedRAMP's rules. The agency proves its own system and its side of the boundary. A provider's package is presumed adequate, but the agency still authorizes its own system, and only its authorizing official accepts the remaining risk. Next, the clock.

## Slide 3 — The federal clock: the dates still ahead

The dates driving this work come from FedRAMP, CISA and OMB, and each one binds a different party. Every date on the slide was checked against its issuing document, and where a document gives a period instead of a date, the date is counted from it. The nearest one is the post-quantum migration plan, due October 22, 2026. Logging plans, CISA's remediation timelines and FedRAMP's vulnerability rules follow over the next few months, and FedRAMP's 2026 rules become mandatory on January 1, 2027. Further out, there's TLS 1.3 support by 2030. Some things also moved. Software bills of materials became a contract choice, but the software and hardware inventory is still required. The practical advice is simple: keep one dated register, and re-verify it at every gate. Now, what does Moderate actually mean?

## Slide 4 — Moderate: two facts, not one

Most civilian cloud systems are authorized at Moderate, and that word is doing real work. The agency rates the impact of losing confidentiality, integrity and availability, and the highest rating wins. If nothing is high and at least one thing is moderate, it's a Moderate system. The agency then tailors NIST's Moderate baseline, which is the size of the promise that system makes. FedRAMP's Class C is a separate fact. It includes the Moderate baseline, but it lists more controls, a few dozen of them outside NIST's Moderate set. So cite the two separately. A Moderate system built on a Class C service inherits the provider's evidence for the provider's side. It still has to prove its own side, against its own tailored baseline. Which raises the real question: when is a control actually closed?

## Slide 5 — A control closes only with its proof

Is a control closed? A program that can't answer precisely answers loosely: the product was bought, the vendor announced the feature, the ticket was marked complete. None of those is the control operating. Book 14 applies two tests, in order. First, the mechanism exists: it's deployed, running, and reaches the part of the estate the control covers. A purchase order or a roadmap fails. Second, its proof is recorded: a machine-readable verification, a time, and what checked it. Fail the first test and the control is open, with a milestone in the plan of action. Pass the first but fail the second and it's worse: it looks closed and can't be shown to be. And a vendor roadmap is not a commitment. Date every claim, and write the capability into the contract. Next, the families no provider can close for you.

## Slide 6 — Program management: the agency's, never the provider's

Some control families no cloud provider can ever close for you, and program management is the first. NIST treats the whole family as organization-wide, outside any system baseline, and FedRAMP doesn't assign it to any class. It comes from FISMA and OMB, and it binds the agency whether or not it uses a single cloud service. No tool can write an organization's risk tolerance or appoint its senior official. These controls close when the artifact exists, a named official has approved it, and the agency can show it's still followed. Think of the security program plan, the appointed senior security officer, a signed risk tolerance, and one organization-wide monitoring strategy. One distinction matters to an assessor: the program inventory lists systems, and each system's inventory lists what's inside it. The two must agree. Two more families close the same way.

## Slide 7 — Contingency and supply chain close through governance

Contingency planning and supply chain also close through governance, not technology. Contingency planning asks for a plan, not a capability. You can have tested failover, two circuits and good backups, and still carry an open finding, because nothing says who declares a contingency, who gets told, what comes back first, or when you're back to normal. And the mission owner, not the database administrator, sets the maximum tolerable downtime. For a Moderate system, NIST recommends a real exercise with a restore from backup, not just a tabletop. Supply chain shifted this year. The secure-development attestation mandate was rescinded, and software bills of materials became a contract choice. But the inventory is still required, and supply chain policy and controls are in every baseline. Providers report their vulnerabilities; the agency reads those reports into its own plan of action. Next, privacy and training.

## Slide 8 — Privacy and training close by records, not settings

Privacy and training belong to the agency, end to end. The privacy baseline applies at every impact level, so a Low system holding the same personal information carries the same privacy controls, and FedRAMP doesn't cover any of them. The questions are about authority and purpose: why the agency collects the information, whether it told the person, and whether it published the system of records notice. The senior privacy official approves the privacy plan before authorization. Training is the one family no configuration can close. Training comes before access, and awareness is refreshed at least yearly. Here's the trap: a dashboard showing ninety-eight percent completion is a number with no denominator. The proof is counts per population, measured against a stated roster, that someone who didn't produce them can re-derive. So who owns all of this?

## Slide 9 — One owner for every step

Ask who owns compliance and the answer is often the security office, which really means nobody in particular. The Risk Management Framework names an owner for every task. The system owner carries the most: categorizing, implementing controls, fixing findings, keeping the plan of action, and assembling the package. The CISO sets requirements and the common controls systems inherit. The privacy official approves anything touching personal information. The authorizing official is the only one who can accept the risk, and that can't be delegated. They also approve the plans and select the assessor before the assessment starts. Several of these roles are government-only, so a contractor-heavy team can prepare every plan and package, but can't sign those decisions. And keep the roles apart: nobody assesses their own work. Now, the package itself.

## Slide 10 — The package: four linked, machine-readable records

The authorizing official doesn't read the evidence directly. They read a package that summarizes it: the security and privacy plans, the assessment reports, the plan of action, and an executive summary. NIST says the package may be generated from a tool, and FedRAMP's 2026 rules now expect machine-readable artifacts, at least OSCAL and JSON. In OSCAL the pieces link together: the assessment plan points to the security plan, the results point to the assessment plan, and the plan of action hangs from the security plan. Every failing item gets a disposition. Real drift is an open item with an owner and a deadline. An accepted exception carries its expiry, and when that lapses, it's open again. Nothing is always satisfied. And an empty plan of action on a Moderate system should make you ask what the assessment didn't look at. Next, keeping it current.

## Slide 11 — Continuous monitoring keeps the package current

A package describes the system on the day it was generated. Continuous monitoring keeps that day recent. The law sets the floor: testing at least annually, and there's no federal three-year rule. Once monitoring is mature, ongoing authorization replaces a fixed end date with a review frequency. The program's own cadence is monthly, quarterly and annual. Each month, regenerate the results and plan of action and report posture to the authorizing official. Each quarter, review every provider's ongoing report, with a named senior official doing it. Each year, an independent assessor re-assesses every control. One caution: vendor posture scores aren't assessment evidence. A score measures progress against a vendor's recommendations. An assessment result says whether an objective is satisfied, and on what evidence. Let's step back to the map.

## Slide 12 — The series as a map, and what stays open

The older Federal Compliance series arranged its material by layer. The workbook arranges it by the question a reader brings, and a chapter maps each part of the series to the book that carries it now. Where they disagree, the book is the later, footnoted text. Two ideas explain the order. Naming and identity come first, because you can't inventory what you can't name. Assurance comes last, because the package can only report what the earlier books made observable. And even after you build everything on the map, risk remains. The slide lists six kinds, from self-assessment that no independent assessor has confirmed yet, to features not yet shipped. Write that remainder down. A package that says what's proven, asserted, inherited and accepted gives the authorizing official a risk they can see. That leads to the decisions.

## Slide 13 — Decisions to make, and next steps

So, four decisions. Name the owners in writing, with signed appointments and the authorizing official selecting the assessor. Keep one dated clock: submit the post-quantum migration plan, and re-check every federal date against its source at each gate. Fund the mechanisms that produce records before the tools that measure them, because assessment tools confirm a design; they can't stand in for one. And generate the package, with a disposition on every failing item and a named reviewer for every provider's quarterly report. The next step needs no procurement. Fill in the product inventory questionnaire against the estate as it is today, and settle every flag before the security plan is written. A promise is true on the day it's signed. Proof keeps showing itself. Book 14 has the detail, with every claim footnoted. Thank you.
