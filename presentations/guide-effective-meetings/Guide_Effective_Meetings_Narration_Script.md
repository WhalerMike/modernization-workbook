# Companion guide — Effective Meetings — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — Effective Meetings

Welcome. This briefing comes from a companion guide to the Federal Modernization Workbook, and it's about something every program does every week: meet. The guide starts from one idea. A meeting is a tool for deciding and coordinating. If it ends without decisions and owners, it was a status report that cost an hour of everyone's time. I'll follow the same four questions as the book decks: why meetings matter, what a good meeting needs, who does what in the room, and how to run one from before it starts to after it ends, with two templates and a checklist at the end. Let's start with why this matters to a modernization program.

## Slide 2 — A modernization program runs on its meetings

Why should a modernization program care about meeting habits? Because meetings are where the program is actually coordinated. From the change advisory board to the cutover go or no-go call to the steering committee, most of the program's decisions get made in a meeting of some kind. And the guide makes a sharper point: most of a program's slippage starts in a meeting that ended without a clear decision or a named owner. So a meeting that drifts isn't just a lost hour. It can be the moment a schedule starts to slip, with nobody holding the next step. The good news is that the fix isn't new.

## Slide 3 — The habits are old, and they work

These habits have a long track record, and that's the best reason to trust them. At Intel, a course on effective meetings was mandatory for new employees, and Andy Grove often taught it himself while he was C E O. In government, G S A's eighteen F, the federal digital-services team, published a short public guide built on the same few ideas: a clear purpose, a complete agenda, assigned roles and written next steps. And university faculty-development and engineering programs teach the same structure. When industry, government and academia keep landing on one pattern, it's because the pattern works. Here it is in a single picture.

## Slide 4 — Before, during, after: what each phase produces

It helps to see the whole routine at once, because each phase depends on the one before it. Before the meeting, you set it up: the purpose, the agenda, the roles and the right people. During it, you run it: on time, on the agenda, with anything off-topic parked. After it, you follow through: written notes the same day, and every action with an owner and a due date. The part to notice is the arrow back. What comes out of one meeting opens the next one's agenda, so nothing quietly drops. But there's a question that comes before all of this: do you need a meeting at all?

## Slide 5 — Is a meeting needed at all?

Every meeting spends its attendees' time, so the first question is whether to hold one. Eighteen F's test is simple: is this meeting a better use of everyone's time than not meeting? A faculty-development session at a children's hospital in Pittsburgh looked at why people actually call meetings. Some reasons are good ones, like giving information or working a problem. Others are more honest about habit: satisfying requirements, or simply tradition. Its advice is to ask whether some of those goals could be met another way. The guide turns that question into a simple working rule.

## Slide 6 — Meet, write it down, or retire it

This rule matters because it gives people permission not to meet. It's the guide's own design guidance for the program, built on those two sources. If information only needs to flow one way, send a written update, and meet only if the questions really need live discussion. If there's a problem to work or a decision to make, that's what meetings are for, so meet, with the right people in the room. And give every recurring meeting a check once a quarter. One that no longer decides anything should be cancelled, or turned into a written report. Once you've decided to meet, the next question is what kind of meeting it is.

## Slide 7 — Three kinds of meeting, and what each is for

Knowing the kind of meeting tells people how to take part. Engineering guidance from Portland State University, drawing on Cameron Herold's book Meetings Suck, sorts meeting work into three types: sharing information, solving a problem, and making a decision. Each one needs something different. Sharing works when the material goes out in advance. Problem solving needs a time limit, a note-taker, and a phase that opens up ideas before one that narrows them. And a decision needs the question, and the way the group will decide, agreed beforehand. One meeting can hold more than one type, but each agenda item should be one of them, and the agenda should say which. The same source adds two warnings.

## Slide 8 — Don't do the work in the room; name the shift

These two warnings land hard on technical programs, where it's tempting to solve the problem live, on the call. The first is: don't do the work in the meeting. When the group starts doing detailed analysis together, stop, agree who will do it, by when and what they'll deliver, and then go back to the agenda. The second is: name the shift. When the discussion moves from reporting into problem solving, or from problem solving into deciding, the facilitator says so out loud. It keeps everyone working on the same task at the same moment. Both warnings depend on a good agenda, and that's the next topic.

## Slide 9 — The agenda defines the meeting, and its end

The agenda matters more than most people think, because it defines what the meeting covers and tells everyone when it's over. When the agenda is done, the meeting is done. A list of topic names can't do that job; a line that just says network tells nobody what's expected of them. A real agenda writes each item as a question or an outcome, says whether it's there to inform, to discuss or to decide, and gives it an owner, a time and any preparation. Which three pilot sites go first? focuses a room far better than pilot sites. Around those items, a few more details make the difference.

## Slide 10 — Around the items: purpose, title, timing, headcount

These details are small, but they're what let people prepare, and decide whether they need to come. Above the items, state the purpose and the expected outcomes, and say where the notes will live. Give the meeting a specific title, like cutover go or no-go, wave two, rather than weekly sync, and keep the agenda to a single page. Send it at least a day ahead, and let people propose changes before the meeting instead of debating the agenda at its start. Eighteen F also suggests fifteen- or forty-five-minute meetings, which leave room between meetings and spare time if an item runs over, and about a dozen people at most. With the agenda set, let's look at who does what.

## Slide 11 — Six roles, and everyone knows which they hold

Roles matter because the guide's rule is simple: every meeting needs named roles, and every attendee should know which one they hold. The table shows the six and what each does, so I'll pick out two. The facilitator focuses on the process, not the content, and doesn't have to be the person who called the meeting; it's wise to have a backup. And the decision-maker has to be in the room. If the person with the authority isn't there, an item marked decide simply can't be decided, however good the discussion was. The role that's forgotten most often, though, is the note-taker.

## Slide 12 — Who's taking notes? Combining and rotating roles

Eighteen F calls failing to assign a note-taker the most common meeting mistake, and it's also the easiest one to fix. Any attendee can simply ask: who's taking notes? Roles don't need a crowd, either. In a small or recurring meeting, one person can hold two of them, the facilitator acting as closer, or the note-taker keeping time. And rotating the roles across the team spreads the skill, so the meeting doesn't depend on any one person. With the roles in place, here's how a meeting runs from start to finish.

## Slide 13 — Four phases, one loop: before, during, close, after

Thinking in four phases helps, because each one produces something the next one needs. The picture earlier showed three; the guide's routine splits out a fourth, the close, a few minutes reserved at the end of the meeting itself. That's where discussion turns into decisions read back and actions with owners and dates. Before gives you an agenda and the right people; during gives you the discussion and the decisions; and after puts everything in writing and carries it into the next agenda, which closes the loop. Let's take each phase in turn, starting before anyone joins.

## Slide 14 — Before: confirm, write, assign, remind

Much of a meeting's success is settled before it starts. First, confirm the meeting is needed, and what type it is. Then write the agenda and send it with the preparation attached, assign the roles, and invite only the people the items need. The children's hospital guidance gives a good test for that invitation list: the people affected by a decision, and the people with the knowledge, resources or authority it requires. Shortly before the meeting, send a reminder with the agenda, then arrive early and test the equipment. Now the meeting begins.

## Slide 15 — Start on time, and stay on the agenda

The first few minutes set the tone for everything after them. Start on time, every time. Open by stating the purpose, confirming the agenda and naming the roles, and point people to the notes, slides and chat you're using. Agree on a few ground rules, such as take space, make space, so the air time is shared. From then on, follow the agenda, and leave it only deliberately, for a set time. Summarize as you go. And when someone arrives late, point them to the agenda and the notes rather than recapping for them. So what happens to the topics that don't fit? That's the job of the parking lot.

## Slide 16 — The parking lot, and notes worth keeping

A parking lot lets you protect the agenda without dismissing anyone's point. Topics that are off the agenda, or can't be settled in the time, go somewhere everyone can see, like a section of the notes or a corner of the whiteboard. Each one gets an action, and the whole lot is reviewed before the meeting ends. That follow-up is what keeps people willing to park a topic at all. Notes work the same way. Capture the meaning, not the dialogue, put decisions and assigned tasks first, and fill in any gaps the same day. Then comes the close.

## Slide 17 — Close: read back decisions, owners and dates

The close is where discussion becomes commitment, so it gets its own place on the agenda: the last few minutes, with the aim of ending early. Read back the decisions. Then read back each action item with its owner and its due date, and have each owner confirm it out loud. Route every parking-lot item, ask what's missing and what to change about the meeting next time, and set the next meeting and its facilitator if one is needed. Then the work moves outside the room.

## Slide 18 — After: notes the same day, no meeting after

Follow-through is where a meeting's value is kept or lost. Send the notes and action items in writing, with owners and dates, the same day. Store them wherever the team keeps its records, and make any correction visible to everyone who attended. Then open the next meeting by reviewing the previous action items. And avoid the meeting after the meeting. Decisions made in the hallway leave out the remote attendees, and they never reach the notes. To make all of this routine, the guide gives two templates.

## Slide 19 — Template: the agenda

A template matters because it makes the good agenda the easy one to write. This is the guide's agenda template. The top block carries everything we covered earlier: a specific title, the length, a one-sentence purpose, the expected outcomes and where the notes are kept. Then the roles, by name, including the decision-maker. Each item is a question, with its type, owner, minutes and preparation. Look at the first and last rows: every meeting opens by reviewing the open action items, and ends with a reserved close. The second template records what happened.

## Slide 20 — Template: notes and action items

The notes template is built around what people need afterward, and that isn't a transcript. After a short header with attendees, absentees and roles, it has four parts. Decisions, each with who decided and how. Action items, each with an owner, a due date and a status. The parking lot, with where each item is routed. And open questions, each with who will answer, and by when. It ends by setting the next meeting and its facilitator. Everything we've covered also fits on a single page.

## Slide 21 — The one-page checklist

A checklist matters because it puts every habit in one place you can check quickly. This one follows the same moments we've walked through: before the meeting, the start, during it, the close, and after. Each row is a handful of quick checks, from whether a meeting is needed at all, to the decision-maker attending, to the action items being reviewed at the next meeting. It's the whole guide, on one page. For anyone who wants to go deeper, two books are worth the time.

## Slide 22 — Further reading, and the standard in one line

The two books go deeper than any guide can. Andy Grove's High Output Management is his own account of managing at Intel, and it includes a chapter on meetings. Cameron Herold's Meetings Suck is the source of the roles and meeting types in Portland State's toolbox. But the standard itself fits in one line: a real agenda, named roles, a parking lot, and written action items with owners and dates.
