# Book 16 — People & Training: the executive briefing — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — People & Training: the executive briefing

Welcome. This is the executive briefing for Book 16 of the Federal Modernization Workbook, People: Training and Education. It's for the CIO, the chief human capital officer and HR leadership, the CISO, the workforce and training leads, and the program's sponsors. Tools enforce the new rules, but people make the decisions those rules depend on, so the modernization isn't finished until people do their jobs the new way. I'll take four questions: why people close the gap, what the curriculum teaches, who owns readiness, and how to run and measure it. Each decision slide names the decision, its owner and when it's needed. Let's start with the gap itself.

## Slide 2 — The tools enforce; people make the decisions

A policy can require phishing-resistant sign-in for every user. It can't decide what to do when a caller says they've lost their key and need in now. That's the gap this book is about. The new estate removes many risky settings, and what's left is a smaller set of human decisions that carry more weight: is this caller who they say they are, is this access justified, is this exception still needed. Someone who doesn't know the new rule falls back on the old habit. So the decision for the program office: put a training check, measured against a roster, into every gate, beside the technical criteria. Next, whose work changes.

## Slide 3 — Every book moves a decision to someone new

Every book in this workbook moves a decision to someone. The help desk now verifies before it resets. Administrators activate their roles only when they need them. Owners approve access, and HR keeps the data that creates accounts right at the source. Jobs split into deciding and doing, so two people now need each procedure. And decisions move to people outside IT, who need short training on the one decision that's now theirs. So the first workforce decision belongs to the chief human capital officer's office: draft the roster of everyone whose work changes, from HR and identity records, before the first gate. Next, why timing matters.

## Slide 4 — Training scheduled last is paid for twice

A program that trains last pays twice. The first payment comes at cutover: users who weren't taught the new sign-in call the help desk, and a desk that wasn't taught the new procedure either turns people away or resets accounts without the checks. Workarounds granted under pressure tend to stay. The second payment comes later, finding those workarounds and unlearning the habits, which is slower than learning them. And the rules already say train before the change, not after. So the decision for the sponsor and CIO: fund the learning program as part of the modernization, before the first gate. Next, what federal workforce rules actually require.

## Slide 5 — What federal workforce rules actually require

Federal workforce rules are less about courses than people expect. FISMA asks for trained personnel sufficient to comply, a staffing statement, and enough for the old estate may not be enough for the new one. A 2015 law requires every agency to identify and code its cyber positions. OPM's rule sets awareness training before access and at least once a year, plus training whenever systems change significantly. And none of the civilian rules requires a particular certification. So the decision follows Circular A-130: the chief human capital officer and the CIO set competency requirements together, and feed the program's new roles into the workforce plan. Next, the position codes.

## Slide 6 — Coded positions are the cleanest roster source

Position codes matter because they're the cleanest way to know who needs role training. But they've been unreliable. In 2019, GAO found that 22 of 24 agencies had given about one in five of their IT management positions a non-IT code, and over half of sampled codes didn't match the duties. The program uses the code as one input to the roster, beside role assignments and the system inventory, and treats any disagreement as a finding for HR and the CIO's office. So the decision, as program design: HR classification and the CIO's office review the affected position descriptions and their codes as each workstream reaches its gate. Next, what the curriculum actually teaches.

## Slide 7 — Education first, then a track for every role

Before anyone learns a tool, the program teaches a way of thinking: six ideas, starting with governance as something that runs all the time, and identity as the starting point. Someone who holds them sees why the help desk now asks for proof. Someone who doesn't feels every new rule as friction. Then training teaches the job, in four tracks: compliance for the people who read evidence, implementation for the people who build, vendor training and labs for product skills, and short modules for every user. The decision for the chief human capital officer's office: approve a training plan that names an owner for every track, by the first gate. Next, the engineers.

## Slide 8 — Engineers: a build is done when its evidence lands

Engineers get a track built on one rule: a build isn't done when it works, it's done when its evidence lands where an assessor can read it. Three habits come with it. Watch before you enforce, so a policy runs in report-only before it can lock anyone out. Keep a way back in, with emergency accounts created first. And export the record, never a screenshot. Each build is paired with the compliance module that reads its evidence. The decision for the steward, who owns the foundation gate: no gate passes until the engineers for the next phase have passed their labs. Next, how the program knows training worked.

## Slide 9 — Certify what people can show, not attendance

How do you know training worked? Not from attendance. The program certifies what people can show. Each track ends in a capstone, scored from missing up to assessor-ready, and the top level means a real, dated result. Two program credentials come out of it, one for practitioners and one for assessors, and both expire after a year. Industry certifications still count as evidence of general skill, but they can't stand in for the agency's own procedures. The decision for the CISO is a design option from the book: once the training record is reliable, decide whether a current credential becomes a condition for privileged roles. Next, where people practice.

## Slide 10 — Labs: practice everything, touch nothing real

People need somewhere safe to practice, and that's harder to arrange than it sounds. Many staff won't qualify for a developer sandbox on their own, and those sandboxes don't exist in the government clouds at all. So the program's approach has two steps: practice in a commercial sandbox, then rehearse once in the agency's own non-production government tenant before each gate, where a feature difference shows up as a finding instead of a surprise. Every lab follows the same rules: never production, never real data. The decision for the program office: arrange lab tenants centrally, including that government rehearsal tenant, before the first labs. Next, the largest group of all.

## Slide 11 — Every user signs in differently by the last wave

Every user will sign in differently by the end of the program. They'll learn how either from the agency, before the change, or from an attacker, after it, because someone who's never seen the new prompt can't spot a fake one. So end users get six short modules, each ending in something they do: sign in with a phishing-resistant method, recover an account, enroll a device, find their files, report something suspicious, and know what the help desk will never ask. The decision belongs to the CISO: what users are taught about sign-in, reporting and the help desk, because those words are the policy users actually see. Next, the help desk.

## Slide 12 — The help desk is trained first, and is a target

The help desk is where the new estate is most exposed. Attackers have phoned employees pretending to be IT, and phoned the help desk pretending to be employees. So the desk is trained first, before anyone else migrates, and no technician gets the power to reset a credential until their training and an observed verification scenario are on record. Users learn the desk's rules too: it will never ask for your password or a code, and it'll always let you hang up and call back on the published number. The decision sits with the service desk manager: whether the desk is trained and staffed for each wave. Next, how waves are paced.

## Slide 13 — Waves move on readiness, not on the calendar

Waves need a rhythm. Users hear about the change about two months ahead, get their training a month out, and get reminders in the last two weeks. Then, before anything is enforced, there's a readiness check on three numbers: how many users registered a new sign-in method, how many finished training, and how busy the help desk is. When tickets climb, the guidance is to slow down. The wave owner, identity engineering, makes the go or no-go, with training now part of it. And the rule is simple: a wave that isn't ready moves its date, not its threshold. Next, who owns all of this.

## Slide 14 — Who owns workforce readiness: one decider per row

Ask who owns training and usually three offices answer: security, HR and the CIO's office. And nobody owns the thing that matters: whether people can do the job on the day it changes. The book gives each decision one owner. The CISO owns the training policy and what users are taught. The chief human capital officer's office owns the workforce strategy, the position codes and the roster's facts. The decision for the sponsor and CIO: name one training lead, and bring in the human capital office, labor relations and the Section 508 program manager at the first gate, not after the first complaint. Next, who decides readiness.

## Slide 15 — The training lead reports; others decide

Readiness numbers are easy to improve without improving readiness. So the book keeps the roles apart. The training lead reports the numbers, the system's security officer reviews them, and the gate or wave owner decides. Managers can't waive required training, and phishing test results are for learning, not discipline. Contractors can build, deliver and report all of the training, but the decisions stay with government officials. And one decision sits with the authorizing official alone: whether to accept the risk of running a system before its people are trained. That's a risk acceptance like any other. Next, how the curriculum runs on the roadmap.

## Slide 16 — Training runs on the roadmap, phase by phase

Training has to run on the roadmap's calendar, not the training office's. Each phase has people who must be ready before its gate: leaders oriented before planning, platform teams and the help desk trained before the foundation is built, and every module tested on the pilot. And there's one step most programs miss: when Active Directory retires, every course and runbook that teaches it retires too, or people keep learning a system that's gone. The decision for the program office: no gate gets scheduled without its training dates, and no wave without its training window, so when a wave slips, its training slips with it. Next, keeping the knowledge.

## Slide 17 — Keep the knowledge when people and contracts change

Training is only half the job if the knowledge walks out the door. The book builds in a few safeguards. Each module has an owner from the team that runs the platform, and at least two qualified instructors, so no one person holds it. Administrators of retiring systems are trained into new roles, or reassigned, before the old platform goes. And contractors are on the same footing as staff: their training goes into the agency's own learning system, even when their employer trained them. So the decision for contracting officer's representatives: make sure every contractor's training is on the agency's record before they get access. Next, how readiness is measured.

## Slide 18 — Readiness is measured on the job, not in the course

Finishing a course isn't the same as being ready. So the program measures three things. Completion, for everyone. Competence, for every role where a mistake is a security event, like the help desk and privileged administrators. And behavior, from signals outside the learning system: whether users actually registered a new sign-in method, whether help desk tickets settle back down after a wave, and what people say a few weeks later. The pilot sets the baselines, and each later wave adjusts them. The decision for the system's security officer, against the CISO's threshold: anyone overdue in a privileged role is a finding, whatever the percentage says. Next, what leadership should see.

## Slide 19 — What the steering committee should see

Leadership needs a short, regular view, not a dashboard nobody acts on. At every steering committee meeting, one page answers five questions: is the next gate's workforce ready, are the next two waves ready, what happened in the last wave, where are the gaps in privileged roles, given as a count rather than a percentage, and what does the committee need to decide. Each quarter there's a fuller report, and once a year the evaluation OPM requires, of what the training cost and what it changed. The decision: the steering committee reviews readiness at every meeting, and the head of the agency owns the annual evaluation. Finally, the decisions in one place.

## Slide 20 — Decisions to make, owners and timing

So, the decisions in one place. The sponsor and CIO fund the learning program and name a training lead. The program office puts training into every gate and onto the roadmap. The chief human capital officer's office owns the roster, the training plan and the position codes. The CISO decides what people are taught. The help desk manager and the wave owner decide when people are actually ready, and the authorizing official alone accepts the risk if they aren't. None of this is a procurement. The first step is simple: draft the roster of everyone whose work changes. Tools enforce; people decide. Book 16 has the detail, with every claim footnoted. Thank you.
