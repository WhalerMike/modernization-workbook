# Companion guide — Non-Person Identity — Implementation, Part 1 — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — Part 1: Inventory, decisions and credential rules

Welcome to Part 1 of the implementation series for non-person identity. When a person leaves, HR tells you. When a service account's owner leaves, nothing tells you. So this track has to build the record that HR provides for everyone else. The guide does that in six gates. For each gate, we'll cover what done means, the steps, the commands from the developer workbook's first labs and what they really print, the federal requirement and the evidence an ISSO keeps, what's available in the government clouds, and how to roll it back. Part 1 takes the first three gates: inventory every non-person identity, decide each one's target, and set the credential rules. Part 2 takes least privilege, conversion and the lifecycle, and then SQL Server, AI agents, evidence and rollback. Design guidance is labeled as such. And the workbook's code is illustrative. It's tested against fixtures, not against a live tenant.

## Slide 2 — The six gates on one page: done when, proved by

Here are all six gates on one page. The first is done when every non-person identity is listed, typed, and joined to a record with an owner and a sponsor. The second is done when every identity has a target and a date, with high-risk accounts first. The third is done when the tenant blocks new secrets and caps credential lifetimes, and existing credentials sit in a vault on a rotation calendar. Part 2 picks up from there. High-privilege grants are justified or removed. Converted identities sign in without stored secrets, after a clean parallel run. And the paths for owner changes, orphans, reviews and decommissioning run and leave records. Each gate names the read-only validation kit checks that prove it. One note on numbering. The workbook's chapters use the project plan's gates. Its Gate 0 and Gate 1 both fall inside the guide's first gate, and its Gate 2 is the second.

## Slide 3 — Before G1: the record, the roles and the licenses

Before the first gate, agree the record that every non-person identity joins to. For this track, the source of truth is the owning system's record, not HR. The system inventory is the master, and that's design guidance. Each record carries a system or application ID as the join key. Entra apps point at it through a service management reference, which Microsoft recommends because it survives one person leaving. The record also names two technical owners, and a sponsor who is a federal employee. It holds the identity's type and object ID, its target and decision date, its credential and where that's vaulted, and a risk tier. Collecting all this needs reader roles only. The checks sign in with a certificate or a managed identity, never a secret. Most of this track is free. Workload ID Premium adds Conditional Access and ID Protection for workload identities. And in GCC, Entra ID runs in Microsoft's commercial cloud.

## Slide 4 — G1: inventory every kind of non-person identity

The first gate starts with a warning. Consent creates service principals, and Microsoft's own services provision more. So the inventory is always larger than the list of apps anyone asked for. In Active Directory, the read finds user accounts used as services, and the managed, group managed and delegated managed service accounts. It records passwords that never expire, password age and service principal names. Treat stale as a lead, not a verdict, until sign-in logs confirm it. Record which hosts can retrieve each group managed account's password. That list is its real blast radius. In Entra, record each service principal's type. A legacy one can hold credentials with no app registration, so an app-only review never sees it. List managed identities with their role assignments. User-assigned ones outlive their resources, so they're the type that orphans. Export every federated credential's issuer and subject. And list AI agents, including the ones that run as ordinary service principals.

## Slide 5 — G1: join each identity to a record, owner and sponsor

Next, every identity joins to a record in the system inventory. For an app registration, use its service management reference where it's set. Otherwise, search by name and owner, and then interview people. Find the application owner before anyone touches the account. An account nobody will own becomes a retirement candidate. Every app registration and service principal gets two human owners, never service accounts. Keep that list short, because an owner can add a credential and act as the app. The business approver goes in the registry instead. Every identity also gets a sponsor, who is a federal employee mastered in HR. A contractor can build or run a workload, but can't be its only sponsor. The Federal Modernization Workbook sets that rule for agents, and the program applies it to everything here. And where the tenant doesn't expose the agent features, govern agents as the service principals they run as.

## Slide 6 — G1 in code: one register, and the HIGH-risk rows

The workbook's first chapter builds the register in code. It starts from Microsoft Learn's own commands and export script, kept as pinned copies, and adds what they leave out. Its lab runs on a fictional estate, with no tenant and no domain. One run reads both directories and writes one register. Here it finds thirty-five identities, fifteen in Active Directory and twenty in Entra. Eight are high risk. There are eight findings, eleven warnings, and three accounts handed to the Data Platform plan, because they belong to SQL Server. The second command lists the high-risk rows. A sync account's password hasn't changed in twenty-eight hundred days. A backup account sits in Backup Operators. A web front end is trusted for unconstrained delegation. And one app holds a directory-wide write permission. One account was found only because its password never expires, so it may be a person, and someone confirms that first.

## Slide 7 — G1 in code: two human owners and the orphan plan

The second chapter checks ownership against that register. It reads each owner once, and counts an owner only if it's a user, a member, and enabled. A guest or another service principal doesn't count. Against the fictional estate, it checks twenty-two identities and finds seven orphan candidates. Two apps have no valid owner, and four have only one. Six have no service management reference, and three point at a record that's free text, retired or unknown. Five Active Directory accounts have no valid application owner. Then it writes the orphan rule as a plan, and sends nothing. There are ten actions, and three are writes: two disables, and one owner added from a claim. Another claim is refused, because it named a guest. A reviewer approves the plan by its hash. The writes themselves belong to the orphan rule in gate six, which Part 2 covers.

## Slide 8 — G1: requirement, evidence, availability, rollback

Here's the federal side of the first gate. Account management treats non-person identities as a defined account type, each with an owner and a sponsor. The evidence is this gate's record, plus the kit runs for owners and agent sponsors. Identifier management means each identity has its own identifier, joined to its record and never reused. To validate, four read-only checks run. One lists Active Directory accounts whose passwords never expire or are too old, each joined to a record or listed as unowned. One reports zero apps with no owner, and a dated finding for any with just one. One lists every managed identity and its role assignments. And one lists agent identities with their sponsors, where the tenant exposes them. The inventory's Graph reads are listed for the government clouds. Agent identities are generally available in commercial, and in GCC with documented gaps. And there's nothing to roll back, because the inventory only reads.

## Slide 9 — G2: five targets, decided by type and where it runs

The second gate gives every identity one of five ends. Retire it. Convert it to a group managed service account, or a delegated one on a Windows Server 2025 domain. Or move it to a managed identity, a federated credential, or a certificate. The workbook's migration matrix decides by account type, and by where the workload runs. No stored secrets. Managed identities for workloads in Azure. Federation where the workload already has a trusted issuer, such as GitHub Actions, Kubernetes, Google Cloud or AWS. And certificates only where neither applies. A managed service account is upgraded to a group managed one first, because neither can move to a delegated account later. An app with a client secret moves to a managed identity, then federation, then a certificate. High-risk accounts go first. And plan ahead for group managed accounts. They need a key distribution root key, created once for the whole forest, and domain controllers can wait up to ten hours after it's created.

## Slide 10 — G2 in code: propose, check each decision, plan waves

The workbook's third chapter holds the matrix as reviewed data, and keeps three things apart. The proposal is the matrix's target for each account. The disposition is the application owner's decision: retire, re-platform, or keep, with an end date. And the gate is the ISSO's. The lab runs the first two chapters, then the matrix. Twenty-one identities are in scope, and twenty get a proposal. Eighteen of twenty-two have a decision, and two waves are planned. The gate isn't met, and the findings say why. A high-risk sync account sits in wave two, not wave one. One account has no decision. An account kept on Kerberos has no owner for its service principal name. A kept delegated account has no end date before Active Directory retires. One app has no proposal, because nobody recorded where it runs. Wave two has no rollback owner. And a multitenant app is on the premium list, though Conditional Access can't cover it.

## Slide 11 — G2: requirement, evidence, availability, rollback

The guide's evidence table maps by requirement, not by gate. So which rows this gate feeds is our reading. The decisions set the order that authenticator management asks for: managed identities and federation first, and no new secrets. Retire decisions lead to disabled accounts, which account management asks for. And the fourth phase of the workbook's directory migration is its gate for service accounts. To validate, every row in the record has a target and a decide-by date. The kit check for Active Directory accounts shows every high-risk one in the first wave, or retired. The check for secrets lists each app that holds one. Each has a credential target, or an exception that says why it can't move. Workload ID Premium is available in Azure Government through the account manager, but its free trial isn't. The interim path is an account kept on Kerberos, islanded, with an owner and an end date the authorizing official accepts. To roll back, re-decide. The record keeps the history.

## Slide 12 — G3: the tenant policy blocks secrets, caps certificates

The third gate sets the credential rules. They're design guidance from the books. No long-lived secrets. Managed identities first, then federated credentials, then certificates. And every certificate in a vault, on a rotation calendar. Step one turns on the tenant's default app management policy. It blocks new password secrets and symmetric keys, and caps new certificates at a hundred and eighty days. Those are Microsoft's recommended settings. Each restriction applies only to apps created after a chosen date, so set it to the policy's effective date. The panel shows the workbook's diff against a fictional tenant. Its default policy is off, so turning it on is a change, and every restriction is an add, for both application and service principal objects. A restriction the tenant already had is kept, never dropped. Existing workloads rotate at least yearly while they're fixed. New ones start under the cap, which addresses the SCuBA certificate policy.

## Slide 13 — G3: the vault, the upload, and secrets by exception

The rest of the third gate is about where credentials live. Keep private keys in Azure Key Vault or a hardware module, never on a file share. Key Vault renews a certificate automatically only when it's self-signed, or comes from a partnered authority, DigiCert or GlobalSign. Certificates from an agency's own authority aren't renewed, though the vault can email before they expire. And a renewed certificate in the vault doesn't change the app registration by itself. Microsoft's own procedure for SQL Server on Arc uploads the new certificate as a separate step. So automate that upload, or make it a named owner's task with a date. A client secret is an exception. The governance office and the CISO approve it, it lasts at most six months, and it's never renewed automatically. A traditional account that has to stay gets a long random password in the vault, an owner and a date. And a new federated credential is a credential change, approved and recorded like any other.

## Slide 14 — G3 in code: compare the policy, plan the rotation

The workbook's fourth chapter holds the policy as a reviewed file, compares it with the tenant, and plans the difference. Against the fictional tenant, the diff has six adds and one change. The plan has eight writes: the default policy, one dated exception policy and its assignment, and the property lock on five apps. Nothing is sent, and the write path can't loosen or drop a restriction. Then the rotation plan joins Key Vault to the app registrations. A rotation is raised sixty days before expiry, and an owner approves it. The vault makes the next version. The new certificate goes on beside the old one. The owner confirms the workload signs in with it, and seven days later the old one comes off. Here, one certificate renews in the vault, one is attached, and one is removed. One app's certificate isn't in the vault at all, and three apps still hold secrets. Secrets are replaced, not rotated.

## Slide 15 — G3: requirement, evidence, availability, rollback

On the federal side, authenticator management is the main requirement. The evidence is the policy export, the kit runs for credentials and secrets, and the rotation calendar. The policy also addresses three SCuBA policies: no app passwords, and capped password and certificate lifetimes. They're SHOULD policies in ScubaGear, not required configurations, and their report is filed with the gate. Audit requirements cover credential changes, so the entries for added credentials are logged and reviewed. To validate, the credential check shows nothing issued after the effective date that runs longer than the cap. Every credential that expires within sixty days has an owner and a renewal date. The secrets check shows each app still on a secret is in a conversion wave, or has an exception with an end date. App management policies are free, and Microsoft lists their calls for the government clouds. To roll back, give one application a policy exception with an end date, in the exception register.

## Slide 16 — Handing G1–G3 to Part 2

To close Part 1, here's what each gate leaves behind, and where Part 2 picks it up. The first gate leaves the record, with owners, sponsors and risk, plus the register and the SQL Server hand-off. Gate four restores a removed grant only from that export. Gate six runs the orphan rule. The second gate leaves a target and a date for every row, the waves and their rollback, and the premium license scope. Gate four puts the most important single-tenant service principals under workload policies. Gate five converts wave by wave, with a thirty-day parallel run. The third gate leaves the policy, the rotation calendar, the exceptions and the SCuBA result. Gate five drops a secret only after the new credential carries real traffic, and gate six checks credentials every week. To build Part 1's code, use the developer workbook, Building Non-Person Identity, chapters one to four. Each has its labs and a narrated tour.
