# Book 07 — PKI & Certificates — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — Why Do Certificates Outlive Every Migration?

Welcome. This briefing is Book 07 of the Federal Modernization Workbook, PKI and Certificates. It covers the move from Active Directory Certificate Services to Microsoft Cloud PKI, Entra certificate-based authentication and the Federal PKI. Here's why it deserves its own book. Every other migration changes which system answers the question, who is this? Certificates are how that answer gets proved. So when they're handled as an afterthought, things break long after everyone thought the work was done. We'll go in four steps: why certificates outlive every migration, what the target PKI looks like, who owns the certificate estate, and how to move it without stranding a single certificate. Let's start with the why.

## Slide 2 — Certificates fail at expiry, not at cutover

Certificates outlive migrations because a certificate isn't a record in a directory. It's a signed statement that's already been handed out. When the directory moves, the certificate doesn't. It keeps working until it expires, and then whatever relied on it stops. And a lot relies on it: domain controllers, smart card and PIV logon, Wi-Fi and VPN, TLS listeners like SQL Server, code signing and encryption. That gives the program two clocks. On the directory's clock, cutover is declared complete, and months later the first expiry wave finds no issuer, far from its cause. On the certificate's clock, the replacement issuer is live before the first expiry, and AD CS retires last. The program runs on the certificate's clock. And the rules certificates live under are already changing.

## Slide 3 — The rules are moving under certificates already issued

A certificate estate used to be something you could leave alone between renewals. Three changes have ended that. First, public TLS lifetimes are shrinking on a fixed schedule. The maximum fell to 200 days in March 2026, drops to 100 days in March 2027, and to 47 days in March 2029. On the book's arithmetic, a thousand certificates means about seven renewals every working day today, and about thirty-one at 47 days. Second, the rules for mapping certificates to accounts changed under certificates already in use. Domain controllers moved to full enforcement in 2025, with no way back. Third, the algorithms have a horizon. NIST's draft plan would deprecate RSA-2048 after 2030 and disallow RSA and ECDSA signatures after 2035. Put together, manual renewal can't keep up, so the move off AD CS runs as its own program. First, what does AD CS hold up?

## Slide 4 — What AD CS holds up, and the four verdicts

Before you can move AD CS, you need to know what it holds up, and it isn't just one server. It's a hierarchy of CAs, a set of forest-wide objects like templates and the NTAuth store, and Group Policy autoenrollment on every domain member. The inventory has four layers: the CAs, the templates, the certificates actually issued, and the relying parties. The relying parties set the order of work, because a new issuer is useful only once everything that checks its certificates trusts it. Then every template and use case gets one of four verdicts. Migrate it to the cloud. Bridge it, running both issuers, where the consumer depends on AD, like domain controllers. Retain it where there's no cloud equivalent, like HSM-held code signing. Or retire it. The book also corrects its source here: internal web and SQL Server TLS can't move to Cloud PKI. And whatever stays has to be hardened.

## Slide 5 — Harden what stays before anything moves

AD CS keeps issuing trusted credentials while the migration runs, so the CA you're leaving has to be the best-defended system you own until it's gone. The book treats hardening as a detect-and-fix checklist of eight known weakness classes. Think of things like templates that let a requester name any subject, or permissions that let the wrong people change a template. The slide lists all eight. Defender for Identity detects most of them, as long as its sensor is on the CA. One class has no automated check, so it's a manual review. The gate is simple. Every Critical and High finding is fixed and verified before any workload migrates, because the recommended cloud issuer chains to this same hierarchy. And none of it has evidence until CA auditing is turned on, which takes two settings together. With the old side secured, what are we moving to?

## Slide 6 — Four issuers, each for what only it can serve

The target isn't simply Cloud PKI instead of AD CS. That's true for devices and wrong for the estate. The target is four issuers, each serving what only it can. Cloud PKI issues to Intune-managed devices, and only to them. A smaller, hardened AD CS stays for domain controllers, RADIUS, internal server TLS, network devices and code signing. The Federal PKI keeps issuing PIV, and the target's job is to trust it. And a public CA serves names the internet must trust. One decision ties them together: the root. For an established hierarchy, the program recommends bring-your-own-CA, where the cloud issuing CA is signed by the existing offline root. That keeps the chain, but it makes the root's revocation list a production dependency. And check the licensing: the 2026 change that bundles Cloud PKI into E5 covers commercial customers, so confirm it for a government tenant. Four issuers need one way to keep them renewed.

## Slide 7 — One lifecycle platform: CyberArk Certificate Manager

Four issuers need one lifecycle platform. Intune and Cloud PKI renew what they put on managed devices. But nothing Microsoft-native does that for AD CS server certificates, public TLS, appliances, or the certificates nobody inventoried. That's the job of certificate lifecycle management: find every certificate, give it an owner, and renew it automatically. The program's platform is CyberArk Certificate Manager. CyberArk is the program's existing vendor, so it consolidates there instead of adding a second one. The names have shifted over the last couple of years, and the notes trace the history. Two facts shape the deployment. There's no FedRAMP listing for the certificate product, so the program runs the self-hosted edition inside its own authorization boundary. And its AD CS account holds certificate-manager rights, so it's scoped and reviewed like any certificate manager. Which raises the people question.

## Slide 8 — Who decides, who does the work for certificates

Who owns the certificate estate today? In most forests, whoever installed the CA. Domain and enterprise admins are CA administrators by default, and nobody ever decided that. The book fixes it by separating deciding from doing. The sponsor and CIO decide the target hierarchy, when each piece of AD CS retires, and who owns renewal for each class of certificate. The CISO and ISSO decide issuance policy, trust anchors and the minimum binding strength. The authorizing official accepts standing exceptions. On the doing side, PKI administrators run the CAs, the identity team owns the Entra trust store, endpoint engineering owns the Intune profiles, and the lifecycle platform owner runs discovery and server renewal. The principle is separation of duties. Whoever can change a CA must never be the only person who decides what it issues. Now, how the certificates actually get delivered.

## Slide 9 — Intune delivers the certificate, strongly mapped

Start with devices. Intune delivers certificates three ways, and each needs the trusted root profile deployed first. SCEP from Cloud PKI is the cleanest: the device makes its own key, with no on-premises server in the path. SCEP from AD CS runs through an NDES server and a connector, so its permissions are kept tight. PKCS makes the key on the connector server and exports it, which is why the program prefers SCEP. The big change is strong mapping, and it's no longer optional. Domain controllers have been in full enforcement since 2025, and there's no way back. A certificate used against a domain controller without a strong mapping is simply rejected now. So the SCEP profiles add the user's or device's on-premises security identifier to the certificate. Pilot that in a new profile, because editing an existing one revokes what it issued. That's the device side. On the user side, Entra checks certificates.

## Slide 10 — Entra CBA validates certificates; it issues none

Entra certificate-based authentication is a relying party. It checks certificates and issues none, so its risk runs the other way: a certificate that should fail might pass. Trust and revocation come first. Upload every CA in the chain, give each one a revocation list Entra can reach quickly, and turn on Require CRL validation. A CA uploaded without a CRL lets revoked certificates keep working. Then binding, meaning how a certificate is matched to a user. Some bindings are high affinity, like issuer and serial number. Others, like a name or email, are low, and the old guide got one of those wrong. A certificate counts as multifactor only when a PIN or biometric protects it, like a PIV card. Production requires high affinity, because CBA is only as strong as its weakest binding. But only switch once every user's certificate IDs are right, or they're locked out. Next, running both PKIs at once.

## Slide 11 — Run both PKIs, then migrate in six gated waves

For most of the migration, both PKIs are in production. Devices carry certificates from both issuers, and relying parties trust both chains. Three rules make that safe. Trust goes up before issuance moves. Nothing is revoked to force a move; old certificates leave at renewal. And every revocation list must be reachable from inside, from the internet and from Entra, because most coexistence outages are really revocation outages. The migration runs in six gated waves, about forty-four weeks on the program's planning figures for a single forest. It goes from assessment, through a foundation and pilot, to scaling across every managed device, cutting over sign-in, and finally decommissioning what's no longer needed. The slide shows each wave's gate. Every wave has a rehearsed rollback, and AD FS stays restorable for thirty days after cutover. But the migration ending isn't the end.

## Slide 12 — Keep it renewed and checkable, then retire AD CS last

Migration ends on a date; certificates don't. With public TLS lifetimes heading to 47 days, no certificate class can depend on someone remembering a date. Three renewal engines cover the target. Intune renews managed devices. AD CS autoenrollment renews domain controllers and RADIUS while the forest stays. And CyberArk Certificate Manager renews servers, appliances and public TLS, using ACME where the endpoint supports it. Monitoring watches for warning signs, not outages: a certificate with no renewal path, a stale revocation list, issuance outside policy. The checks run at least weekly, and their history is the evidence. Retiring an AD CS CA comes last, and the slide walks through the steps. The key one is easy to forget: keep its revocation endpoints serving until the last certificate it issued expires. Under bring-your-own-CA, the root that signed the cloud issuer stays. So, what do we need decided?

## Slide 13 — Decisions to make, and next steps

Here are the four decisions. First, confirm the root: bring-your-own-CA, signed by the offline root, once the hierarchy passes the hardening gate. Second, settle the Cloud PKI licensing for the government tenant, rather than assuming the commercial change applies. Third, stand up the self-hosted CyberArk Certificate Manager inside the authorization boundary as the single lifecycle tool. And fourth, name the owners: issuance policy and trust anchors with the CISO, and one renewal owner for every class of certificate. The next step is wave one, Assess. That's four weeks to inventory every CA and template, close the Critical and High findings, classify every use case, and list every relying party. Run certificates on their own clock, and the old CAs can retire without stranding anything. Book 07 has the detail, every claim footnoted, with deployment checklists and a template-by-template matrix. Thank you.
