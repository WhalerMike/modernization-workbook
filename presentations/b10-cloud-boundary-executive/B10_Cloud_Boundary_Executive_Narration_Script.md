# Book 10 — Cloud Boundary & Landing Zone — Executive Briefing — narration script (practice copy)

## Slide 1 — What Does the Authorization Boundary Let You Use?

Welcome. This is the executive briefing for Book 10 of the Federal Modernization Workbook, Cloud Boundary and Landing Zone. It's for the people who sign for a cloud system or answer for it: authorizing officials, ISSOs, CIOs and CISOs. The book's argument fits in one line. The license says what you bought; the boundary says what you may use. A design that confuses the two fails in front of the assessor, not in the lab. Over the next thirty minutes I'll take four questions. Why the authorization boundary decides what you can use, and why so much of it stays invisible. What changed at FedRAMP in 2026, and what Government Community Cloud leaves out. Who owns the boundary. And how to respond when a capability falls outside it, and evidence that response in your own package. There's no configuration on these slides. Each decision slide says what the decision is, who owns it, and when you need it. Let's start with two agencies and one license.

## Slide 2 — Three checks, three owners decide what you can use

Picture two agencies with the same Microsoft 365 license. One runs a commercial tenant, and its admin center shows per-location network assessments, informed network routing and an Adoption Score. The other runs a GCC tenant and sees none of them, because Microsoft documents each as unavailable there. Nothing in the license or the price list tells the second agency that. The book's explanation is that three separate decisions, by three different parties, have to line up before a feature is usable. FedRAMP certifies a specific offering, and the Marketplace is the record. Microsoft decides what each government environment includes, and publishes the differences one feature at a time, with no single list. And the agency's authorizing official accepts the risk of the agency's own use. What you can actually use is the overlap of all three. So the first decision is simple and cheap. Before the next architecture review or authorization milestone, require every cloud feature a design depends on to be listed with its Marketplace package, its availability in your environment, and the page that says so. Workload owners declare the dependencies; the system owner checks each one. A feature with no citation is unverified, not available. Next, why nobody sees this coming.

## Slide 3 — Why the gap stays invisible, and the example corrected

Why does nobody see this coming? The book gives four reasons. Commercial and government tenants share the same admin centers and much of the same documentation. Exclusions are scattered, usually one sentence on each feature's own page. Features move, so a gap list from 2025 can be wrong today. And absence doesn't announce itself. A detection whose input never arrives looks just like a quiet day. Build a Zero Trust control on a signal your environment doesn't deliver, and you haven't built a weak control. You've built no control, and nothing tells you so. But the opposite error is just as costly. A widely repeated example listed five features as silently missing from GCC. Checked against Microsoft's documentation, only one is. The others are gaps in higher environments, aren't documented gaps, or belong to Azure's own scope. An unverified boundary story sends an agency to build compensations it doesn't need. The program's own register of GCC telemetry gaps has 26 rows. Eight are vendor-confirmed; fifteen are inference. So rebuild any gap list you rely on so every row cites a vendor page. The system owner keeps it, and the CISO decides what counts as evidence. Next, what changed at FedRAMP.

## Slide 4 — FedRAMP 2026: services are certified, and you still authorize

FedRAMP announced its Consolidated Rules for 2026 on June 25, 2026. A cloud service offering is now FedRAMP Certified, at a class from A to D. Class C includes the current Moderate baseline, and Class D the current High. Two cautions come with the new labels. The class describes what FedRAMP assessed, not your use; agencies may authorize a service at whatever security category they deem appropriate. And certification doesn't replace your decision. Each agency still authorizes its own systems, and notifies FedRAMP afterward. The dates matter for planning. Adoption is mandatory for everyone on January 1, 2027. FedRAMP stops accepting new Rev5 certification requests on June 11, 2027, and says the end date for existing Rev5 certifications isn't final, but is likely no later than 2029. Plan against those words, not against a specific date someone repeats. So for years you'll hold two kinds of package at once. Rev5 packages describe a boundary the provider drew. Packages under the new rules describe scope, as data. Read each in its own terms, and don't force one vocabulary into the other. Next, what the word boundary now means.

## Slide 5 — "The boundary" is now four sets of obligations, and yours

The rules also changed what the word boundary means. In the current machine-readable ruleset, the phrase authorization boundary appears zero times. A provider's boundary is now four sets of obligations: what's in scope, cryptography, vulnerability response, and how evidence reaches you. The only boundary the rules name is the agency's own system. The scope test is about data, not network diagrams. A resource is in the offering if it's likely to handle federal customer data or affect its security, wherever it sits. So a refusal that rests on raising the whole environment's level now has to show which resource and which data. One line matters to every estate. Software installed on agency systems, typically agents, application clients and mobile apps, is entirely outside FedRAMP's scope. That doesn't make it unassessed. It makes it yours. So decide who owns each agent and client, and cover it in the agency's own authorization: its configuration, its updates and the data it sends. The authorizing official draws the boundary; endpoint engineering does the work. Settle it at the next package update, and before the rules become mandatory on January 1, 2027. Next, how changes are handled.

## Slide 6 — Change is notified, not re-approved, and evidence becomes a feed

Under the old model, a significant change to a certified service went back through approval. The new rules replace that with notification, scaled to risk. Routine changes like patching need no formal notice at all. Bigger changes are notified within set business-day windows, and the most transformative ones need plans filed weeks before work starts. A change of certification class still needs a new assessment. Evidence becomes a feed too. Providers must run a FedRAMP-compatible trust center with programmatic access to all certification data. On October 21, 2026, only 110 of 781 Marketplace listings had a trust-center entry. There are two consequences for you. A provider that ships continuously can treat most releases as routine and file nothing, so keeping current with commercial no longer costs it a compliance cycle. But your review duty doesn't shrink. Agencies must review each ongoing certification report for changes that affect the risk they accepted. So name the reviewer now. FedRAMP recommends a federal senior information security official; in the program's model that's the CISO or a named delegate. The ISSO reads each notice against the boundary record, and the authorizing official decides whether it changes the agency's risk. Next, the feature gap itself.

## Slide 7 — GCC is the worldwide service, minus a published list

Microsoft's own documents don't describe GCC as a dimmed version of the commercial service. GCC is the worldwide service, minus a published list of exclusions. The restricted, separately networked environment begins at GCC High. And that list isn't a ladder. Several features are missing in GCC, GCC High and DoD alike, so moving up doesn't restore everything. The largest documented loss is the network connectivity layer. A commercial tenant gets, at no extra license cost, a daily network score for each location, a view of its egress and front doors, insights such as detecting TLS interception, and performance attributed to named network providers. An agency choosing between SASE or transport providers in a commercial tenant gets that comparative evidence from Microsoft, in its own admin center. The same agency in GCC gets none of it. Microsoft's connectivity test tool does run in GCC, and it closes part of the gap, but not the provider comparison. So this decision is a budget line. Fund the independent measurement that replaces that layer, before you pick a transport or SASE provider. That's the sponsor and CIO's call, because they fund the platform. Next, why a higher certification won't fix it.

## Slide 8 — A higher certification class won't bring the features back

Much federal reasoning runs like this: GCC is Moderate, Moderate is why the feature is missing, so a High certification would bring it back. The Marketplace doesn't support that chain. Read on October 21, 2026, it lists three Microsoft 365 government packages. The GCC package agencies run is certified at Class C, Moderate, with 100 authorizations. A second package with the identical name is In Process at Class D, High, with none. And GCC High is a separate package, already certified at Class D. The Marketplace doesn't say how those first two relate, so treat it as a question for the provider, not a roadmap. Here's the key point. Network connectivity is missing in GCC High too, and GCC High is already Class D. A higher class changes what data you may put in the service. It doesn't, by itself, restore a feature the provider excluded. An agency that defers an equivalent because High is coming may wait for something the certification was never going to deliver. So plan against the class of the package you actually use, and don't hard-code Moderate into a multi-year architecture either, because that may move. The sponsor and CIO own that plan, in the current architecture and acquisition cycle. Next, who owns the boundary.

## Slide 9 — One decider per row: who owns the boundary

The book's rule is one decider per row, and it starts from FedRAMP's split. The provider owns the service boundary and its certification. The agency owns the boundary of its own system, and everything it builds, configures and connects inside it. On the agency side, the authorizing official determines the boundary, with input from the system owner. The official signs every exception and decides whether a change needs reauthorization. That risk acceptance can't be delegated, and the role is for government personnel only. The system owner keeps the boundary record and the package. The CISO sets the control requirements and decides what counts as evidence, and the ISSO reads provider notices. The sponsor and CIO choose the clouds, fund the platform and break the ties, which will happen, because everyone can reasonably claim the landing zone. Then the people who do the work. The platform team builds the landing zone, but doesn't decide whether a gap is acceptable. Workload owners declare the features their designs depend on. And only a contracting officer can change what the agency bought. FedRAMP is consulted, not in charge. Its certification is an input to the authorizing official's decision, never a replacement for it. Next, decisions that hide as tickets.

## Slide 10 — Five decisions that look like engineering tickets

The most consequential boundary decisions don't look like boundary decisions. They look like engineering tickets, and that's how they slip past the people who should make them. Take placement. Vending a new subscription is a platform task, but whether a workload is inside the authorized system is the system owner's call. The cheapest way to turn an authorized system into an unauthorized one is a subscription no one recorded. So a workload whose subscription isn't in the boundary record doesn't go live. Then the contract. GCC coverage requires a GCC SKU, so a licensing choice is a boundary choice, and it belongs to the contracting officer. Provider changes that might affect the agency's risk go to the authorizing official, not whoever is on the distribution list. And agencies can't demand more than FedRAMP requires without a formal finding of need. The landing zone makes these decisions checkable, but it doesn't make them. Two planning facts for leaders. Microsoft's landing-zone accelerators are supported only in public Azure, so an Azure Government build needs the code adapted by hand. And the pipeline that builds it is part of the boundary question too, because its state data is sensitive. Plan both; don't discover them. Next, what to do when a capability is outside.

## Slide 11 — Three honest responses when a capability is outside

When a capability is outside the boundary, the book gives three honest responses, each a decision with an owner. One: compensate inside the boundary. Rebuild the capability, or enough of it, from signals you do have, and say plainly that it's derived. For the network gap, scheduled runs of Microsoft's test tool close part of it, labeled derived. A narrative that hides the substitution is exactly the kind an assessor takes apart. Two: a documented exception. Either a request through the provider's process, or the authorizing official accepting the residual risk in writing. A gap you intend to close goes on the plan of action and milestones, with an owner. A gap you'll live with gets a signed exception with an end or review date, so that permanent is a decision someone repeats, not a default. Three: an alternative path, a different certified service. And going without is legitimate too, as long as someone decided it. Before any of these, probe. Test what actually works in your own tenant, on a schedule and after relevant provider notices. So for each gap, the system owner proposes a response, the authorizing official decides every exception, and the CISO sets the evidence class, before a design depends on the capability. Next, two cautions on exceptions.

## Slide 12 — Exceptions: the provider's path, and the rules' limits

Two cautions on exceptions. First, the provider's path. ExpressRoute for Microsoft 365 is the worked example. Microsoft doesn't recommend it, and authorizes it only when a regulation mandates direct connectivity, quoted, with a link. Federal readers often assume FedRAMP or FISMA supplies that hook. The program hasn't found a rule or provision that does, and TIC 3.0 mandates no specific technology. Write it into a task order before the authorization is in hand, and you may find the gate afterwards. But the process is a good template for any request: a named requirement, quoted, with a link. Second, the scope arguments the 2026 rules allow each come with a limit. Agents are outside FedRAMP, but not outside your authorization. Routine changes go unnotified, but you still decide whether they affect your risk. And acquisition is where an exception path gets closed by accident. Microsoft's Network Provider Program is no longer open to new providers, so a solicitation that requires membership restricts competition to a closed pool. Specify the capability, not the product that happens to meet it today. The system owner decides whether to make a provider request, and that decision belongs before the solicitation is written. Next, the alternative path.

## Slide 13 — The alternative path: change the suite, keep the identity plane

The third response is a different certified service for the workload. The book works through collaboration, and its first rule is to read the certification, not the announcement. On the Marketplace, Google Workspace is certified at Class D, while Gemini for Government is a separate listing at Class B, Low, even though Google says Gemini in Workspace apps is FedRAMP High authorized. Which features sit inside which certification is a question for the package, and details like that decide what you can turn on. The public record shows both outcomes. In September 2025 a cabinet-level department signed a five-year agreement worth up to 89 million dollars to move more than 50,000 employees to Google Workspace, with a temporary 71 percent price reduction. So a five-year model has to price the renewal years. A military department went the other way, ending about 150,000 Google email licenses after an audit found about 16 percent in use. Measure adoption in the pilot; don't assume it. The choice is only safe if the identity plane stays put. Entra ID remains the source, and the suite consumes identity, with Conditional Access applied at sign-in. The sponsor and CIO make the suite decision as its own approval, separate from identity modernization, and read the listing again on the day they decide. Next, what to ask your provider.

## Slide 14 — What to ask a cloud provider for

The book turns all of this into requests. Give us commercial parity can't be answered. A specific question tied to a published rule or exclusion can, with a yes, a no or a date. Three habits make a request hard to deflect: name one item, cite the basis with a link, and ask for data, not documents. There are eight requests, and the themes are clear. Publish the exclusion list as data, and name the exception path for each item. Restore the network observability layer in GCC, or publish the obstacle. Explain the two same-named GCC packages. List the agents and clients outside the certified offering. And deliver a trust center and change notices in machine-readable form. On October 21, 2026, none of the three Microsoft 365 government packages had a trust-center entry. Five of the eight rest on obligations the 2026 rules already place on providers, so none needs a boundary argument. And asking isn't requiring: requiring more than FedRAMP does takes a determination by the agency head or a delegate. The system owner makes these requests, with acquisition, once the feature inventory exists, and records each request and answer in the authorization package. A documented no is evidence too. Finally, your own package.

## Slide 15 — Evidence the boundary in your own package

An agency never authorizes a cloud service; it authorizes its own system, which uses one. The provider's certification is evidence you read. Your package is evidence you write, and it should contain only what the agency is responsible for. FedRAMP is blunt: don't copy and paste provider controls into your plan, and don't blindly copy provider findings into your plan of action and milestones. The program writes the boundary down once, as a record the system owner keeps, and every artifact cites it. The system description names the boundary. Each external service is named with its package, class and SKU. Each crossing gets an agreement. When something changes, one record changes, and every narrative that cites it stays current. Know whose package you're reading. The GCC listing says it leverages Azure Government as its infrastructure, so inheritance runs through two provider packages before it reaches you. A compensating narrative takes four sentences: what the control needs, what the provider documents as unavailable, what runs instead, marked derived, and where the remaining gap is recorded. The system owner builds this before the package reaches the authorizing official, and keeps it current from then on. To close, the five decisions.

## Slide 16 — Five decisions, five owners, and the next step

So, five decisions, each with its owner. The authorizing official determines the boundary before workloads arrive, and signs every exception with an end or review date. The system owner keeps the boundary record, gates every new subscription, and checks every feature dependency before a design is built. The CISO names the reviewer of provider change notices and decides what counts as evidence, with the ISSO doing the reading. The sponsor and CIO fund the measurement and compensations the register calls for, and plan against the class you actually have, not the one you're waiting for. And the contracting officer buys the SKU the boundary assumes, and specifies capabilities, not program badges. The next step needs no award. Before the next architecture review, list every cloud feature your designs depend on, with its package, its availability in your environment and the page that says so. Then put the book's requests to your provider, and record the answers. The rules become mandatory on January 1, 2027, and new Rev5 requests stop on June 11, 2027. Neither date moves your own boundary. It's still yours to draw, to evidence and to sign. The license says what you bought; the boundary says what you may use. Book 10 has the detail, with every claim footnoted. Thank you.
