Chapter 11 — The Authorization Package and Continuous Monitoring

What an agency authorization package contains under NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2, the OSCAL model for each artifact, the assessment plan and the program’s test plans, assessment results, the plan of action and milestones lifecycle under CA-5, continuous monitoring under CA-7 and SP 800-137, ongoing authorization, and what FedRAMP’s 2026 continuous-monitoring rules ask of providers and of the agencies that use them.

An authorization package is not a binder that gets signed every few years; it is four linked records, kept in a machine-readable form, that stay true for as long as the evidence behind them keeps arriving.

NoteIn this chapter

What the authorization package contains and which Risk Management Framework task produces each part. The OSCAL model for each artifact and how the models link. The assessment plan, the program’s catalog of test plans, and what assessment results must say. The plan of action and milestones as a lifecycle with dispositions rather than a list, with illustrative code that builds one in OSCAL. Continuous monitoring under CA-7 and SP 800-137, ongoing authorization, and what FedRAMP’s 2026 rules ask of cloud providers and of the agencies that review their quarterly reports.

Every chapter before this one ends in an artifact: a contingency plan that was tested, a supply chain register, a privacy notice, a training record. This chapter is where they meet the person who has to decide. The authorizing official doesn’t read the evidence directly. They read a package that summarizes it, and they accept the risk the package describes. If the package is assembled by hand once every few years, it describes the system on the day it was assembled. If it is generated from the evidence the systems produce, it can describe the system today.

Two framing facts govern everything below. First, the agency authorizes its own system. When that system uses a FedRAMP-certified cloud service, the provider’s certification is evidence the agency reads, not a decision the agency inherits; Book 10, Chapter 11 covers how the boundary with the provider is written into the package, and FedRAMP’s rules require the agency to complete its own authorization process before using a certified service.1 Second, the agency’s package is built from evidence, not from statements about evidence. The evidence contract that every source emits, keyed on authoritative asset identity plus time, is Book 12, Chapter 02; why a system-produced record outranks a signed statement is Book 13, Chapter 07. This chapter is the compliance view of the same machinery: what the package must contain, and how it stays current.

What the package contains

NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2 defines the package by its contents. Task R-1 says authorization packages “include security and privacy plans, security and privacy assessment reports, plans of action and milestones, and an executive summary,” with more at the authorizing official’s request, and that organizations “maintain version and change control” as the package is updated.2 Each part is the output of an earlier task, and each task has an owner.

Artifact Common name RMF task that produces it Who is responsible
System security plan (and privacy plan) SSP S-6: review and approve the security and privacy plans Authorizing official or designated representative approves; the system owner writes it
Security assessment plan SAP A-2: develop, review and approve plans to assess implemented controls Authorizing official or designated representative, and the control assessor
Security assessment report SAR A-4: prepare the assessment reports documenting findings and recommendations Control assessor
Plan of action and milestones POA&M A-6: prepare the plan of action and milestones based on the assessment reports System owner or common control provider
Authorization package — R-1: assemble the package and submit it to the authorizing official System owner, common control provider, and the senior agency official for privacy for systems that process personal information
Authorization decision ATO or ATU R-4: determine if the risk is acceptable Authorizing official

Source: SP 800-37 Rev. 2, Tasks S-6, A-2, A-4, A-6, R-1 and R-4.3

Four details in those tasks shape the rest of the chapter.

  • The package is meant to be generated. R-1’s expected output is a package “which may be generated from a security or privacy management tool,” and NIST encourages organizations “to maximize the use of automated tools in the preparation, assembly, and transmission of authorization packages.”4 Under ongoing authorization, the package is presented to the authorizing official “via automated reports.”5
  • A comparable report counts. If another report contains what an assessment report or an authorization package must contain, SP 800-37 says that report “would itself constitute” the assessment report or the package.6 That is the license for a machine-readable bundle to replace a document, and the condition on it: the bundle has to hold the content.
  • Risk acceptance stays with one person. “The explicit acceptance of risk is the responsibility of the authorizing official and cannot be delegated to other officials within the organization.”7 Everything the program automates stops short of that decision (Book 13, Chapter 09).
  • The decision carries a date or a frequency. The decision conveys “the authorization termination date or time-driven authorization frequency”; a system under ongoing authorization has the frequency instead of the date.8 The section on ongoing authorization below explains how a system earns that.

The package isn’t submitted to FedRAMP. An agency’s authorization of its own system is its own decision under the RMF. What the agency does owe FedRAMP, when its system uses a certified cloud service, is notice: the 2026 rules require it to notify FedRAMP after authorizing use, with a copy of the authorization letter (Book 13, Chapter 11).

One OSCAL model per artifact

NIST’s Open Security Controls Assessment Language gives each artifact above a machine-readable model. OSCAL arranges its models in three layers: the control layer (catalog, profile and control mapping), the implementation layer (component definition and system security plan), and the assessment layer (assessment plan, assessment results, and plan of action and milestones), which NIST describes as focusing “on assessment activities, on communicating all assessment findings including supporting evidence.”9 The current release is OSCAL 1.2.3, published August 7, 2026, with JSON and XML schemas for every model.10

Package artifact OSCAL model Layer What it must link to
Control baseline (for example the NIST Moderate baseline) Catalog and profile Control The NIST SP 800-53 catalog, which NIST publishes in OSCAL
System security plan System Security Plan Implementation The profile it implements
Reusable component narratives (an in-boundary equivalent, a shared platform) Component Definition Implementation The controls each component supports
Security assessment plan Assessment Plan Assessment The system security plan (import-ssp, required)
Security assessment report Assessment Results Assessment The assessment plan (import-ap, required)
Plan of action and milestones Plan of Action and Milestones Assessment The system security plan (import-ssp) or a system identifier

Sources: OSCAL layers and models page; OSCAL 1.2.3 JSON schemas.1112

The source volume said the POA&M imports the assessment plan. It doesn’t: in the 1.2.3 schema the POA&M’s import is import-ssp, an optional link to the system security plan (with system-id as the alternative), and its only required parts are a UUID, metadata and the POA&M items.13 The chain therefore runs SSP ← assessment plan ← assessment results, with the POA&M hanging from the SSP and pointing at observations and risks. The difference matters to a generator: a POA&M built only from one assessment run’s results loses the items that came from continuous monitoring, audits or earlier assessments, which CA-5 says it must also carry.

Machine-readable form isn’t only good practice. FedRAMP’s 2026 agency rules require that internal governance, risk, compliance and inventory tools “can produce and ingest machine-readable artifacts,” at minimum OSCAL and JSON.14 An agency whose package exists only as documents can’t meet that rule for its own system, and can’t read a provider’s certification data as data either.

The assessment plan and the test plans

CA-2 asks for a control assessment plan that describes the controls under assessment, the assessment procedures, and the assessment environment, team, roles and responsibilities; that the authorizing official or a designated representative review and approve it before the assessment; and a control assessment report that documents the results.15 SP 800-37 adds that assessment plans “reflect the type of assessment the organization is conducting,” naming continuous monitoring and post-remediation assessments among the types, and that approved plans should “employ procedures, methods, techniques, tools, and automation to support continuous monitoring and near real-time risk management.”16 The procedures themselves come from SP 800-53A, which gives each control objectives to examine, interview and test; for CA-5, for example, the objects to examine include the control assessment plan, the assessment report, the assessment evidence and the plan of action and milestones, and the test is of the mechanisms for developing and maintaining it.17

Two features of the assessment plan need care.

Independence. CA-2(1) asks for independent assessors, and it is in the NIST Moderate baseline, as is CA-7(1), which asks for independent assessors to monitor controls on an ongoing basis.1819 A self-assessment run by the system’s own team, however automated, is not the independent assessment. The source volume was candid about this: every verdict its evaluation engine produced was a self-assessment, advisory until an independent security control assessment confirmed it. The program keeps that line. Automated results are evidence the assessor uses; the assessor decides what they show. An agency may engage a FedRAMP-recognized assessment organization as its independent assessor, but nothing requires it to for its own system.

The test plans. An assessment plan names procedures. Something has to say which tests actually run, against what, and what counts as passing. The program keeps a single catalog of every test plan that governs behavior, and three rules keep the catalog honest. They are the program’s design.

  1. Every plan has an entry. No plan lives only on a wiki, in a ticket or in someone’s head. If a plan governs behavior, the catalog names it and points to it.
  2. Every plan declares its pass signal. An executable plan emits an exit code, a structured report or a test-result stream. A review plan names the reviewer and the date. A plan whose pass signal is “seems fine” is an essay, not a plan.
  3. Retired plans stay in the catalog. They’re marked deprecated and point to their successor, so the audit trail survives the program’s own changes.

Four kinds of check run as blocking gates on every relevant change: discovery and drift smoke tests, schema validation of every registry, metadata validation of every authoritative document, and the drift findings the comparison emits. Their results stay in the same evidence chain as everything else. The catalog is what lets the assessment plan cite tests by name, and it is what makes an automated assessment repeatable: a re-run of the same plan against the same evidence gives the same verdict (Book 13, Chapter 05).

Assessment results

The assessment report is the authorizing official’s view of what the assessor found. SP 800-37 says it records findings “necessary to determine the effectiveness of the controls implemented within or inherited by the information system,” and, under ongoing authorization, presents information about deficiencies “(i.e., other than satisfied findings determined by assessors).”20 In OSCAL, the Assessment Results model carries one or more results, each with a start time and the controls reviewed, and each finding names its target and an objective status whose state is satisfied or not-satisfied, with an optional reason of pass, fail or other.21 (The source volume listed other as a third state; in the schema it is a reason, not a state.)

The program’s design adds three properties to each finding, all carried as OSCAL properties and links:

  • The evidence behind it. Each finding links to the observations that support it, and each observation records how it was made (examine, interview or test), when it was collected and the asset it concerns. A finding with no observation is an assertion.
  • The source of the verdict. A configuration result from a conformance scanner, a record from an evidence category the program built, or a manual review. The assessor weighs them differently, and the report says which is which (Chapter 04).
  • The freshness. When the evidence was collected and when it stops being current. Stale evidence produces a finding about the monitoring, not a pass.

A report that shows every control satisfied deserves a second look before anyone celebrates. The source volume reported a clean result against the program’s own rule set while acknowledging that the rule set mapped to fewer than half of FedRAMP’s published indicators. A clean result against a partial yardstick says the yardstick is partial. The program’s rule is that the assessment report states what it measured against and what it didn’t, so the authorizing official sees the coverage, not just the score (Book 13, Chapter 12).

The plan of action and milestones

CA-5 asks for a plan of action and milestones that documents planned remediation of weaknesses or deficiencies found in assessment, and for updates to it at a defined frequency “based on the findings from control assessments, independent audits or reviews, and continuous monitoring activities.”22 SP 800-37’s Task A-6 says what each entry holds: the tasks to be accomplished, with a recommendation for completion before or after authorization; the resources required; milestones; and scheduled completion dates. The authorizing official reviews it “to ensure there is agreement with the remediation actions planned,” and it is then used to monitor progress.23

One sentence in Task A-6 is easy to misread. “Plan of action and milestones entries are not necessary when deficiencies are accepted by the authorizing official as residual risk.”24 That doesn’t make an accepted deficiency disappear. SP 800-37 says in the same paragraph that deficiencies are still documented in the assessment reports, which can be kept in an automated tool “to maintain an effective audit trail.” The program goes further, as a design choice: an accepted deficiency stays in the record as a governed exception, with the acceptance it rests on and a date it expires, so that an acceptance can’t silently outlive the conditions it was granted under.

The disposition vocabulary comes from the evidence contract the series specified, and it is the program’s design. Every item that can fail resolves to one of three dispositions, and no item may be “always satisfied”:

Disposition Meaning POA&M treatment OSCAL risk status
Actionable drift Failing, with no acceptance on file An open item with an owner, milestones and a deadline; blocks the pipeline gate open
Governed exception Failing, covered by an in-date acceptance or a registered compensating decision Recorded with the acceptance reference and its expiry; doesn’t block the gate deviation-approved
Lapsed acceptance Failing, and the acceptance has expired Reverts to an open item; blocks the gate open

OSCAL’s risk status vocabulary is open, investigating, remediating, deviation-requested, deviation-approved and closed;25 the mapping in the last column is the program’s. Two properties of this scheme matter to an assessor. An exception surfaces as a POA&M-linked entry rather than a silent pass, and every expiry is computed from a stated “as of” date, so a re-run with the same inputs gives the same dispositions. And an item closes only when re-test evidence shows the condition gone, not when a ticket closes (Book 12, Chapter 12). Where a gap closes by configuration, by a compensating control or by acceptance is the gap closure register in Book 13, Chapter 12.

The source volume called a zero-item POA&M “the correct target state.” The program doesn’t. An empty POA&M on a moderate system means either that every control is effective, which is unusual, or that the assessment didn’t look in the places where the findings are. The authorizing official should ask which.

The script turns each finding into an OSCAL observation, a risk and a POA&M item, linked by UUID, with the program’s own fields carried as properties in an agency namespace.

"""Build an OSCAL plan of action and milestones from evidence-contract findings.

Illustrative only. Input: a JSON list of failing findings, each already carrying
a disposition. Output: an OSCAL 1.2.3 POA&M document. Validate the output against
NIST's published JSON schema before relying on it.
"""
import json
import sys
import uuid
from datetime import datetime, timezone

NS = "https://agency.example.gov/ns/fmp"   # the agency's own property namespace
STATUS = {                                  # disposition -> OSCAL risk status
    "actionable_drift": "open",
    "lapsed_acceptance": "open",            # an expired acceptance reverts to open
    "governed_exception": "deviation-approved",
}

def now():
    return datetime.now(timezone.utc).isoformat(timespec="seconds")

def build(findings, ssp_href, system_name):
    observations, risks, items = [], [], []
    for f in findings:
        obs_id, risk_id = str(uuid.uuid4()), str(uuid.uuid4())
        observations.append({
            "uuid": obs_id,
            "title": f"{f['id']}: {f['control'].upper()} on {f['asset_id']}",
            "description": f["summary"],
            "methods": ["TEST"],
            "types": ["finding"],
            "props": [
                {"name": "asset-id", "ns": NS, "value": f["asset_id"]},
                {"name": "evidence-source", "ns": NS, "value": f["source"]},
            ],
            "collected": f["collected"],
        })
        risk = {
            "uuid": risk_id,
            "title": f"{f['control'].upper()} deficiency on {f['asset_id']}",
            "description": f["summary"],
            "statement": f"Control {f['control'].upper()} is not satisfied on {f['asset_id']}.",
            "status": STATUS[f["disposition"]],
            "props": [{"name": "disposition", "ns": NS, "value": f["disposition"]}],
            "deadline": f["due"],
            "related-observations": [{"observation-uuid": obs_id}],
        }
        if f["disposition"] == "governed_exception":
            risk["props"].append({"name": "acceptance-ref", "ns": NS, "value": f["acceptance_ref"]})
        risks.append(risk)
        items.append({
            "uuid": str(uuid.uuid4()),
            "title": f"{f['id']}: remediate {f['control'].upper()} on {f['asset_id']}",
            "description": f["summary"],
            "props": [{"name": "control-id", "ns": NS, "value": f["control"]}],
            "related-observations": [{"observation-uuid": obs_id}],
            "related-risks": [{"risk-uuid": risk_id}],
        })
    return {"plan-of-action-and-milestones": {
        "uuid": str(uuid.uuid4()),
        "metadata": {
            "title": f"Plan of action and milestones: {system_name}",
            "last-modified": now(),
            "version": now()[:10],
            "oscal-version": "1.2.3",
        },
        "import-ssp": {"href": ssp_href},
        "observations": observations,
        "risks": risks,
        "poam-items": items,
    }}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    findings = json.load(open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8"))
    doc = build(findings, ssp_href="ssp.json", system_name="Example moderate system")
    json.dump(doc, sys.stdout, indent=2)

The output for the two findings above validates against the OSCAL 1.2.3 POA&M JSON schema.26 Validation is a step in the pipeline, not an afterthought. One practical trap: the OSCAL JSON schemas use Unicode property escapes such as \p{L} in their patterns, which Python’s standard re module rejects, so a validator built on the jsonschema package needs its pattern check routed through the third-party regex module:

# Validate a POA&M against NIST's published schema for the same OSCAL release.
# Requires the jsonschema and regex packages; the schema file comes from the
# OSCAL release assets (oscal_poam_schema.json).
import json
import regex
import jsonschema
from jsonschema import validators

def unicode_pattern(validator, pattern, instance, schema):
    if isinstance(instance, str) and not regex.search(pattern, instance):
        yield jsonschema.ValidationError(f"{instance!r} does not match {pattern!r}")

Validator = validators.extend(jsonschema.Draft7Validator, {"pattern": unicode_pattern})
schema = json.load(open("oscal_poam_schema.json", encoding="utf-8"))
doc = json.load(open("poam.json", encoding="utf-8"))
errors = list(Validator(schema).iter_errors(doc))
for e in errors:
    print(list(e.path), e.message[:200])
print("valid" if not errors else f"{len(errors)} error(s)")

Three things the script deliberately doesn’t do. It doesn’t decide dispositions: those come from the acceptance register and the evidence, upstream. It doesn’t drop governed exceptions: they appear with their status and reference, which is the point. And it doesn’t merge with earlier POA&M items; a production pipeline keeps item UUIDs stable across runs so an item’s history survives, and carries items from audits and earlier assessments alongside the ones monitoring found.

Continuous monitoring

A package describes the system on the day it was generated. Continuous monitoring is what keeps that day recent. CA-7 asks for a system-level continuous monitoring strategy, implemented under the organization’s, that establishes system-level metrics and the frequencies for monitoring and for assessing control effectiveness, and that includes ongoing assessments, ongoing monitoring of the metrics, correlation and analysis of what both produce, response actions, and reporting of the system’s security and privacy status to defined people at a defined frequency.27 CA-7(4) adds that risk monitoring, covering effectiveness, compliance and change monitoring, be an integral part of the strategy; it is in all three NIST baselines.2829

SP 800-137 gives the program around the control. Organizations “define an ISCM strategy; establish an ISCM program; implement an ISCM program; analyze data and report findings; respond to findings; and review and update the ISCM strategy and program.”30 It is also explicit about automation’s limit: organization-wide monitoring “cannot be efficiently achieved through manual processes alone or through automated processes alone,” and all implemented controls, including management and operational controls, “must be regularly assessed for effectiveness, even if the monitoring of such controls cannot be automated or is not easily automated.”31 A contingency plan test or a training record is as much a part of continuous monitoring as a configuration scan.

The metric set, the pipeline from emission to response and what an assessor examines for CA-7 are in Book 12, Chapter 12. Running the monitoring program as a chartered project across many systems is Book 13, Chapter 12. What this chapter adds is the cadence that keeps the package itself current, and the contract that makes the cadence trustworthy.

The annual floor isn’t optional. The evidence contract’s five rules

The series specified the evidence layer as an open contract rather than a tool, so that any conformant engine, whether a purpose-built governance platform, a commercial governance, risk and compliance product or native OSCAL tooling, can satisfy it. It takes three inputs: a control map in the authoritative record, in which each row ties a requirement to the mechanism that closes it and the evidence category that proves it; evidence records per category; and the cadence and disposition rules above. It produces two outputs: the OSCAL assessment plan, results and POA&M, and the continuous monitoring stream that re-emits them on the cadence. Five rules hold it together. They are the program’s design.

  1. The control map is the authoritative record. No document asserts a closure the map doesn’t carry, and a gate in continuous integration enforces it.
  2. Mechanism, not product. A closure claim names protocol behavior, never a vendor. The product that realizes the mechanism is recorded in the agency’s selection record.
  3. The evidence record is the truth. Narrative never overrides a machine-readable evidence record. If they disagree, the record governs and the disagreement is itself a finding.
  4. Nothing is always satisfied. Every item that can fail carries a live disposition.
  5. OSCAL is the boundary. Outputs are in an open format, so the engine can be replaced without losing the package.

The fifth rule is the one an authorizing official should care about most. A package locked inside one vendor’s tool is a package the agency can’t move, reuse or hand to another agency’s assessor. Book 13, Chapter 08 shows how the program keeps engines and sources replaceable.

Ongoing authorization

No federal rule sets a three-year authorization; authorizations run for periods the agency sets (Book 13, Chapter 01). There is FISMA’s annual testing floor, above, and there is OMB’s direction to move to ongoing authorization: Circular A-130 tells agencies to “transition information systems and common controls to an ongoing authorization process when eligible for such a process and with the formal approval of the respective authorizing officials,” and to reauthorize “on a time- or event-driven basis in accordance with agency risk tolerance.”35

SP 800-37 defines ongoing authorization as “the subsequent (follow-on) risk determinations and risk acceptance decisions taken at agreed-upon and documented frequencies,” time-driven or event-driven.36 An organization may eliminate the authorization termination date when the continuous monitoring program “is sufficiently robust and mature” to give the authorizing official what they need for ongoing risk determination; the authorization frequency then replaces the date, and at each review the official either acknowledges that the risk remains acceptable or says it no longer is.37 The two conditions, the Monitor-step tasks (M-1 to M-6) and the mechanics of the transition are set out in Book 13, Chapter 11. The Monitor step’s own task names say what the cadence above is for: ongoing assessments (M-2), ongoing risk response (M-3), authorization package updates (M-4), security and privacy reporting (M-5) and ongoing authorization (M-6).38

For this book the consequence is practical. The authorizing official can only stop signing a dated decision if the package they review is current, and it can only be current if it is generated. That is why the package, the POA&M generator and the cadence belong in one chapter.

What FedRAMP’s 2026 rules expect

FedRAMP’s Consolidated Rules for 2026 launched on June 25, 2026, and take mandatory effect on January 1, 2027; FedRAMP stops accepting applications for new Rev5 certifications after June 11, 2027.3940 The class pipelines that opened in August 2026 are for cloud providers seeking certification, not for agencies. Their continuous-monitoring rules are written mostly to providers, with a short list of agency duties.

What providers must do. A provider must supply an Ongoing Certification Report to all necessary parties every three months, covering, where applicable, changes to its certification data, planned changes for at least the next three months, accepted vulnerabilities, transformative changes, updated security and configuration recommendations, the agencies using the service, and FedRAMP reportable incidents or an attestation that none occurred.41 Providers at Classes C and D must also host a synchronous Quarterly Review every three months, open to all necessary parties (Class B should; Class A may).42 And FedRAMP’s Vulnerability Detection and Response and Vulnerability Evaluation and Reporting rules become mandatory for every certified offering on December 7, 2026, with certification kept through March 7, 2027 under a corrective action plan and revoked after that for offerings not following them.43 Book 12, Chapter 06 explains how those rules line up with BOD 26-04.

What agencies must do. The rule CCM-AGM-ROR says agencies “MUST review each Ongoing Certification Report” to understand how changes to the service may affect the risk tolerance agreed in their authorization; the agency-use rule AGU-USE-ROR states the same review as SHOULD, and the program follows the stricter MUST (Book 13, Chapter 11).4445 Agencies should consider the security category of their authorization when assigning staff to review reports and attend Quarterly Reviews, and should designate a federal senior information security official to do it.4647 And they must notify FedRAMP if a report, a Quarterly Review or other certification data raises a concern that would likely lead the authorizing official to rescind the authorization.48

The 2026 Key Security Indicators bind providers, not agencies. They are what a provider demonstrates to earn and keep certification; an agency uses them as vocabulary for its own metrics and as an input through each provider’s quarterly report (Book 12, Chapter 12). The source volumes modeled the agency’s own monitoring “on the FedRAMP 20x KSI schema” and counted the agency’s rule set against FedRAMP’s indicators. That comparison is useful as a coverage check and nothing more: the agency’s package is an RMF package, judged against its own baseline by its own authorizing official.

A correction the source got wrong

The source volume built a whole section around a “BOD 26-04 VDR/VER submission”: an SBOM for all production software by December 7, 2026, vulnerability disclosure and exploitability reports filed with CISA by email, and an annual SBOM certification. None of that is in BOD 26-04. The directive, Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk (June 10, 2026), sets risk-based remediation timelines, asset tagging and reporting through the CDM program; it doesn’t mention SBOMs or VEX, and gives no SBOM submission address.49 The “VDR” and “VER” in the source are FedRAMP’s provider rules above, which a provider follows and an agency reads about in the quarterly report. SBOMs themselves are now an agency choice: OMB M-26-05 (January 23, 2026) rescinded M-22-18 and M-23-16, kept the requirement to “maintain a complete inventory of software and hardware,” and lets agencies adopt contract terms requiring a producer to provide a current SBOM on request.50 Chapter 07 covers what that means for supply chain controls.

Vendor scores are not assessment evidence

An authorizing official will reasonably ask why the program builds its own results when the platform vendor already ships posture tooling. The answer is that the vendor tools measure something else. Microsoft describes Secure Score as “a measurement of an organization’s security posture, with a higher number indicating more recommended actions taken,” and a recommended action marked “risk accepted” simply earns no points: the status is a record in the portal, not an item in the agency’s POA&M.51 Microsoft’s own guidance on Compliance Manager says a high compliance score doesn’t mean full compliance: it “doesn’t express an absolute measure of organizational compliance with regard to a particular standard or regulation.”52

Both are useful to operators. Neither is the assessment. A score measures progress against the vendor’s recommendations; an assessment result states whether a control objective is satisfied, on what evidence, judged by an assessor against the agency’s baseline. The program’s design uses vendor scores as operational signals and keeps them out of the package, except where an assessor chooses to examine them. Configuration evidence against CISA’s secure configuration baselines, with dispositions and expiries, is a different thing, and Book 11 shows how it is produced.
