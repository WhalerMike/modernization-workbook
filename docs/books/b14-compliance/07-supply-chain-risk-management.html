Chapter 07 — Supply Chain Risk Management

What the SR family and PM-30 ask for, how NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 frames supply chain risk, where federal policy stands after OMB M-26-05 rescinded the attestation mandate, the SBOM, vulnerability disclosure and VEX evidence pipeline in SPDX, CycloneDX, CSAF and OpenVEX, FedRAMP’s supply chain and vulnerability rules, supplier disclosure, and build provenance.

Most of the code an agency runs was written by someone else, so supply chain risk is closed by knowing what you run, who made it and what is wrong with it, and by keeping that knowledge current.

NoteIn this chapter

The NIST SP 800-53 supply chain controls (the SR family and PM-30), what each asks for and which baselines carry it, and how SP 800-161 Rev. 1 spreads the work across the organization. Where federal policy stands in October 2026, after OMB M-26-05 rescinded the secure-development attestation mandate: the software and hardware inventory is still required, while attestations and SBOMs are now agency choices made through contract terms. The program’s evidence pipeline (software bill of materials, vulnerability disclosure report, exploitability statements) in the formats that matter, the FedRAMP rules that bind cloud providers from December 7, 2026, supplier disclosure, build provenance, and who owns each decision.

A federal system is mostly other people’s software. An operating system, a database engine, a web framework and the hundreds of open source packages underneath it arrive through procurement, package managers and cloud services. Each carries its own flaws, and the agency inherits them on the day it installs the product. Supply chain risk management is the discipline of keeping that inheritance visible: which components are in production, who produced them, which known vulnerabilities affect them, whether those vulnerabilities can be exploited where the component actually runs, and what the supplier has promised to tell the agency when something changes.

The program treats this as an evidence problem, not a paperwork one. The project charter that seeded this chapter states the principles plainly: inventory before you remediate, because you can’t prioritize what you haven’t named; remediation urgency follows exploitability and exposure, not a single calendar; a new software-as-a-service product needs a recorded authorization status before anyone provisions it; component and vulnerability status flow into the evidence stream continuously; and supply chain risk acceptance has a named owner (Book 00, Chapter 06). Those are program design positions. The rest of this chapter shows which of them federal requirements now compel and which the agency adopts by choice.

What the controls ask for

NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 puts supply chain risk management in its own family, SR, with twelve base controls, and adds one organization-wide program control, PM-30.1 The controls are short and parameter-heavy: the organization decides what systems, which suppliers and how often. That leaves the design to the agency and the burden of proof on the agency’s records.

Three points shape the rest of the chapter.

The first is that most of the family isn’t about software at all. SR-9 through SR-12 are about tampering, counterfeits, repair and disposal, which are hardware and property-management questions. The source volume claimed its software pipeline closed SR-10 and SR-11. It contributes evidence to both (hash checks and signatures detect altered software), but anti-counterfeit training, configuration control during repair and component disposal need the agency’s procurement and property processes, and the program records them there.

The second is that SR-1, SR-2, SR-3, SR-5 and SR-8 are in every NIST baseline. Even a Low system needs a policy, a plan, a named team, acquisition language and notification agreements.

The third is that the closing artifacts are governance artifacts. A software bill of materials, a vulnerability report, an exploitability decision, a contract clause and a supplier notification agreement close these controls. No network technology produces any of them. The program says a technology is required only when the control text, physics or protocol behavior makes it the only way to close a control, and saying plainly that supply chain risk isn’t such a case is part of what makes that claim credible elsewhere (Chapter 04).

How SP 800-161 Rev. 1 frames the work

NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1, Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management Practices for Systems and Organizations, is the companion guidance for the SR family. It was published in May 2022 and updated on November 1, 2024.5 It integrates supply chain risk into existing risk management at three levels, each producing different deliverables: at Level 1, the enterprise, the strategy, policy and implementation plan set the tone and boundaries; at Level 2, the mission and business process level, those are tailored to specific missions; and at Level 3, the operational level, system plans determine whether a system meets requirements and carries appropriately tailored controls.6

That maps cleanly onto the controls. PM-30’s strategy is a Level 1 artifact owned by the agency. SR-2’s plan exists at Levels 2 and 3 for each system. SR-3, SR-5 and SR-6 are where Level 1 policy turns into contract terms and supplier reviews. SP 800-161 also expects acquisition strategy to cover approved and prohibited supplier lists and to ensure products “are supplied with a software bill of materials that complies with appropriate agency-approved protocols,” and its supplemental guidance suggests SBOMs for purchased, open source and in-house software.7 For an open source project that publishes no SBOM, it lists three options: contribute SBOM generation to the project, fund the capability, or generate an SBOM on first consumption of each version used.8 The November 2024 update revised the online Appendix F, which carries the software supply chain guidance, to clarify the SBOM language and add the elements of vulnerability advisory reports according to ISO/IEC 29147.9

The federal position in October 2026

ImportantCorrection: the attestation mandate is rescinded

The source volume described a mandatory framework built on Executive Order 14028 section 4, OMB M-22-18 and OMB M-23-16. On January 23, 2026, OMB Memorandum M-26-05, Adopting a Risk-based Approach to Software and Hardware Security, rescinded M-22-18 and its companion M-23-16.10 SBOMs and secure-development attestations are now agency choices made through contract terms, not a government-wide mandate.

M-26-05 is two pages long and worth reading whole. It says each agency head “is ultimately responsible for assuring the security of software and hardware” on the agency’s network, that there is no one-size-fits-all method, and that M-22-18 “imposed unproven and burdensome software accounting processes.”11 What it keeps and what it makes optional are specific:

What M-26-05 requires and what it leaves to the agency.
Item Status under M-26-05
A complete inventory of software and hardware Required: agencies “shall continue to maintain” it
Software and hardware assurance policies and processes that match the agency’s risk determinations and mission needs Required
The Secure Software Development Attestation Form and the other M-22-18 resources Agency choice
A contract term requiring a producer to provide a current SBOM upon request Agency choice; for a cloud platform, the term should require an SBOM of the runtime production environment
NIST SP 800-218 (SSDF), CISA’s 2025 SBOM minimum elements draft, CISA’s hardware bill of materials framework Listed as references for further options

Three related facts have moved with it.

  • Executive Order 14028. Section 4 directed NIST to issue guidance on software supply chain practices, including “providing a purchaser a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) for each product,” and directed Commerce to publish minimum elements for an SBOM.13 It directed guidance; it didn’t itself require any vendor to deliver an SBOM. The source’s claim that it “mandates SBOM for software sold to the federal government” is corrected here.
  • The secure software development framework. Executive Order 14306 of June 6, 2025 rewrote the software provisions of Executive Order 14144, directing NIST to stand up an industry consortium, publish a preliminary update to SP 800-218 by December 1, 2025, and publish a final version within 120 days of the preliminary one; the order’s software provisions say nothing about attestation.14 NIST released SSDF version 1.2 (SP 800-218 Rev. 1) as an initial public draft on December 17, 2025, with comments closed January 30, 2026.15 As of October 21, 2026 the current final publication is still SSDF version 1.1 of February 2022.16
  • The attestation form. CISA’s page for the Secure Software Development Attestation Form, released March 11, 2024, is now marked as archived content that “may not reflect current policy or programs.”17 An agency that still wants attestations asks for them in its contracts.

The program’s position follows directly. The inventory is mandatory, so the SBOM pipeline exists first to serve it: an SBOM is the most precise inventory of software the agency can have. For acquired software the agency decides, system by system, whether to write an SBOM-on-request clause into the contract, and for cloud services it asks for the runtime production environment, as M-26-05’s footnote suggests.18 This matches the charter’s own principle that remediation timelines are risk-based rather than uniform: the federal rule has moved toward the program, not away from it.

The evidence pipeline

The pipeline produces three linked artifacts per release: an SBOM that says what is in it, a vulnerability disclosure report that says which known vulnerabilities affect those components, and exploitability statements that say whether each one matters in this deployment. Each is a machine-readable file keyed to the release, signed and stored as evidence. Book 12, Chapter 02 defines the evidence contract every record follows (asset identity plus time); this pipeline is one producer under that contract.

Formats

Two SBOM formats are in wide use, and CISA’s guidance names both.19

  • SPDX. The Linux Foundation’s System Package Data Exchange specification is recognized as the international standard ISO/IEC 5962:2021; its current version is 3.0.20
  • CycloneDX. OWASP’s CycloneDX covers SBOMs, SaaSBOMs and hardware BOMs as well as vulnerability disclosure and VEX.21 The current version, 1.7, was released October 21, 2025, and Ecma International published it as the second edition of ECMA-424 in December 2025.2223

Two formats dominate exploitability statements.

  • CSAF. The OASIS Common Security Advisory Framework 2.0 became an OASIS Standard on November 18, 2022 and defines five profiles, one of them VEX.24 A CSAF VEX document must give every vulnerability at least one product status (fixed, known_affected, known_not_affected or under_investigation); every product marked known_not_affected needs an impact statement and every product marked known_affected needs an action statement.25
  • OpenVEX. The OpenVEX specification, version 0.2.0, uses the statuses not_affected, affected, fixed and under_investigation. A not_affected statement needs a justification or an impact statement, and an affected statement needs an action statement.26

CycloneDX can carry the same decisions in each vulnerability’s analysis block.27 The program accepts any of the three from suppliers and normalizes them on intake, because CISA’s guidance tells organizations to accept any widely used, interoperable, machine-processable format and to avoid deprecated versions for new software.28

Vulnerability disclosure report

A vulnerability disclosure report (VDR) lists the known vulnerabilities affecting the components in an SBOM. CycloneDX describes the capability as communicating “known and unknown vulnerabilities affecting components and services” and aligns it with ISO/IEC 29147:2018, the same standard SP 800-161’s 2024 update cites for vulnerability advisory reports.4142 The pipeline produces one from each SBOM with a scanner, then joins it to the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog. Every KEV entry carries its own dateAdded, dueDate and forensicTriage fields.43 The deadline that governs the agency’s own remediation is BOD 26-04’s, set by four questions about each vulnerable asset; Book 12, Chapter 06 builds that queue, and this pipeline feeds it rather than computing deadlines of its own.

Watch the acronym. FedRAMP uses “VDR” for its Vulnerability Detection and Response rules, a set of provider obligations. The CycloneDX VDR is a document. The chapter spells out which one it means.

Exploitability statements

A scanner can say a vulnerable component is present; it can’t say whether the vulnerable code is reachable in this deployment. That judgment needs a person, and the program’s process records it:

  1. Generate. The pipeline produces the SBOM and VDR for every release.
  2. Escalate. Any component in the KEV catalog goes straight to the remediation queue and a person reviews it the same day.
  3. Analyze. The owning team reviews each new vulnerability for exploitability in context.
  4. Decide. The team writes a VEX statement with a status and, for not_affected, a justification from the format’s list, or for affected, an action statement.
  5. Sign and publish. The statement is signed, stored with the SBOM and VDR, and shared with anyone downstream who consumes the software.
  6. Review. Every under_investigation and not_affected statement is revisited when the vulnerability record or the deployment changes, and on a fixed cycle the agency sets.

Steps 2 through 6 are program design. The status and justification vocabularies come from the formats; the source’s example used the justification protected_by_mitigating_control, which is a CycloneDX value and is invalid in OpenVEX, whose closest values are inline_mitigations_already_exist and vulnerable_code_not_in_execute_path.4445

Illustrative code

WarningIllustrative code

The workflow, documents, script and query below are illustrative. They have not been tested against a production pipeline or tenant. Action versions, inputs and schemas were checked against their publishers’ documentation on October 21, 2026; pin actions to a full commit SHA, review every step and adapt names, storage and permissions before running anything.

The workflow generates a CycloneDX SBOM on each push to main and each published release, scans it, attests the SBOM’s build provenance, and copies both files to immutable storage. It corrects four problems in the source’s version: cyclonedx-xml isn’t a valid format for the SBOM action (its values are spdx, spdx-json, cyclonedx and cyclonedx-json);46 the scan action is now at version 7;47 storage access used a long-lived SAS token held as a secret, which the workflow replaces with OpenID Connect sign-in;48 and it wrote evidence straight to the Archive tier, which takes the files offline. Immutability is a policy set on the container, not a tier, and every access tier supports it.49

# .github/workflows/sbom.yml
name: SBOM, vulnerability report and attestation

on:
  push:
    branches: [main]
  release:
    types: [published]

permissions:
  contents: write        # SBOM action uploads release assets on release events
  actions: read
  id-token: write        # OIDC for artifact attestations and Azure sign-in
  attestations: write

jobs:
  sbom:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v6

      - name: Generate SBOM (CycloneDX JSON)
        uses: anchore/sbom-action@v0
        with:
          path: .
          format: cyclonedx-json
          output-file: sbom-${{ github.sha }}.cdx.json

      - name: Scan SBOM and write vulnerability report
        id: scan
        uses: anchore/scan-action@v7
        with:
          sbom: sbom-${{ github.sha }}.cdx.json
          output-format: cyclonedx-json
          output-file: vdr-${{ github.sha }}.cdx.json
          fail-build: false   # the gate is the queue in Book 12 Ch06, not this step

      # In a pipeline that builds a deployable artifact, attest the artifact
      # itself and pass the SBOM with sbom-path. This repository-level example
      # attests build provenance for the SBOM file.
      - name: Attest build provenance for the SBOM
        uses: actions/attest@v4
        with:
          subject-path: sbom-${{ github.sha }}.cdx.json

      - name: Sign in to Azure with OIDC
        uses: azure/login@v3
        with:
          client-id: ${{ secrets.AZURE_CLIENT_ID }}
          tenant-id: ${{ secrets.AZURE_TENANT_ID }}
          subscription-id: ${{ secrets.AZURE_SUBSCRIPTION_ID }}
          # environment: AzureUSGovernment   # set for Azure Government

      - name: Copy SBOM and report to immutable evidence storage
        run: |
          for f in sbom vdr; do
            az storage blob upload \
              --auth-mode login \
              --account-name "${{ vars.EVIDENCE_STORAGE_ACCOUNT }}" \
              --container-name sbom-evidence \
              --name "$(date -u +%Y/%m/%d)/${f}-${{ github.sha }}.cdx.json" \
              --file "${f}-${{ github.sha }}.cdx.json" \
              --tier Cool
          done

Notes on the workflow. The SBOM action uploads SBOMs as release assets automatically when it runs on a release, which needs contents: write and actions: read.50 The scan action’s fail-build defaults to true with a medium severity cutoff;51 the program sets it to false here because the remediation deadline comes from BOD 26-04, not from a severity score, and a merge gate belongs in a separate pull-request workflow the team tunes deliberately. GitHub’s attestation action needs id-token: write, contents: read and attestations: write, and consumers verify with gh attestation verify.52 The az storage blob upload command accepts --auth-mode login and --tier.53 The container must already have a time-based retention policy, set to the period in the agency’s records schedule; the source’s flat “seven years (NARA standard)” isn’t carried, because retention comes from the schedule that covers the record.

The KEV join below fixes two bugs in the source’s script (.json and .read were referenced without being called) and reads the CycloneDX JSON report. It records each KEV entry’s own fields and leaves the deadline calculation to the BOD 26-04 queue.

# kev_join.py: flag components in the vulnerability report that are in the KEV catalog
import json
import sys
import requests

KEV_URL = "https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/feeds/known_exploited_vulnerabilities.json"

def kev_hits(vdr_path: str) -> list[dict]:
    kev = {e["cveID"]: e for e in requests.get(KEV_URL, timeout=60).json()["vulnerabilities"]}
    with open(vdr_path, encoding="utf-8") as f:
        vdr = json.load(f)
    hits = []
    for vuln in vdr.get("vulnerabilities", []):
        entry = kev.get(vuln.get("id", ""))
        if entry:
            hits.append({
                "cve": entry["cveID"],
                "components": [a["ref"] for a in vuln.get("affects", [])],
                "kev_date_added": entry.get("dateAdded"),
                "kev_due_date": entry.get("dueDate"),
                "forensic_triage": entry.get("forensicTriage"),
            })
    return hits

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(json.dumps(kev_hits(sys.argv[1]), indent=2))

The field names cveID, dateAdded, dueDate and forensicTriage match the KEV JSON feed.57

The program loads the joined results into a custom table in the security analytics workspace through the Logs Ingestion API (Book 12, Chapter 06 shows the ingestion pattern). A detection that lists KEV-affected components still open past the deadline the queue assigned:

SupplyChainVulnerabilities_CL
| where TimeGenerated > ago(1d)
| where KevListed_b == true
| where VexStatus_s in ("affected", "under_investigation")
| where now() > Deadline_t
| project CVE = Cve_s, Component = Component_s, Deadline = Deadline_t,
          DaysOverdue = datetime_diff('day', now(), Deadline_t)
| order by DaysOverdue desc

The source called now without parentheses. datetime_diff(period, datetime1, datetime2) returns datetime1 - datetime2 in the given period, so the argument order above yields a positive number of overdue days.58 Table and column names are the program’s own.

FedRAMP: what binds cloud providers

For cloud services the agency doesn’t build, FedRAMP sets the supply chain and vulnerability rules, and those rules bind the provider. The agency’s obligation is to consume what the provider reports.

Two of the 2026 Key Security Indicators sit in the Supply Chain Risk theme. KSI-SCR-MIT tells a 20x provider to “persistently identify, review, and mitigate potential supply chain risks,” and KSI-SCR-MON says third-party software information resources “are automatically monitored for upstream vulnerabilities,” through contractual notification requirements or active monitoring services.59 Rev5 providers carry the SR controls listed above for their class.60

FedRAMP’s vulnerability rules are the larger change. Its notice NTC-0014 of June 16, 2026 makes the Vulnerability Detection and Response (VDR) and Vulnerability Evaluation and Reporting (VER) rules mandatory for every FedRAMP-certified offering on December 7, 2026, with a corrective-action grace period through March 7, 2027, after which certification is revoked for offerings not following them.61 The VDR rules define vulnerability detection to include supply chain monitoring, tell providers to remediate KEV entries by the catalog’s due dates “even if the vulnerability has been fully mitigated,” and say providers should not deploy new machine-based resources that carry KEVs.62 The VER rules require providers to evaluate each detected vulnerability for exploitability, internet reachability and potential agency impact, rated N0 to N5, and to report detection and response activity persistently, at least monthly in human-readable form, with JSON for automated retrieval recommended at a frequency that rises with class.63 Book 12, Chapter 06 covers how these rules meet BOD 26-04.

The VER rules also address agencies. Agencies should review provider vulnerability reports at intervals matching their risk posture and should use automated processing of the machine-readable data; FedRAMP’s note recommends reviewing only overdue and accepted vulnerabilities rated above N2 unless the service sits in a higher-risk system. Agencies should use the reported information to maintain their own plans of action and milestones when they mitigate a provider’s vulnerability or accept continued use of a service with accepted vulnerabilities.64 Both are SHOULD rules. The program adopts them as design: the provider’s report lands in the same queue and the same record as the agency’s own findings (Chapter 11).

M-26-05’s cloud footnote completes the picture. Where the agency adopts an SBOM clause for a cloud platform, it asks for the runtime production environment, not the vendor’s client tools.65 The charter’s gate (no new software as a service without a recorded authorization status) checks the service’s FedRAMP certification on the Marketplace and the agency’s own authorization of the system that uses it, as Book 00, Chapter 06 and Book 10 describe.

What to take from this chapter

  1. The SR family closes through governance artifacts: policy, plans, contracts, supplier records, SBOMs, vulnerability reports and exploitability statements. SR-1, SR-2, SR-3, SR-5 and SR-8 are in every NIST baseline.
  2. M-26-05 rescinded M-22-18 and M-23-16 on January 23, 2026. The software and hardware inventory is still required; attestations and SBOMs are agency choices made through contract terms, and for cloud platforms the SBOM to ask for is the runtime production environment.
  3. CISA’s 2026 minimum elements replaced the 2021 NTIA list on July 29, 2026, with seventeen fields; SPDX and CycloneDX are the two formats in wide use, and CSAF and OpenVEX carry exploitability.
  4. From December 7, 2026 FedRAMP’s VDR and VER rules bind certified providers; agencies should consume the reports with automation and fold them into their own plans of action.
  5. Build L3 is hardened builds; two-party review is a separate source-track level. Provenance proves the scanned artifact is the deployed one.
