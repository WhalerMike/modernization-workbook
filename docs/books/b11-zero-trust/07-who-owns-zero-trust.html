Chapter 07 — Who Owns Zero Trust

Who decides, who does the work and who is consulted when an agency sets its Zero Trust posture, runs its assessments and acts on the findings, with the roles of the CIO, the Zero Trust program lead, the CISO, the authorizing official, the pillar owners and security operations, a responsibility matrix and the access each team needs.

Every pillar of a Zero Trust program already has an owner; what most agencies lack is an owner for the posture itself, the one person who can say what the whole-agency score covers, and that person can’t sit inside any cloud provider’s relationship or any single pillar.

NoteIn this chapter

The people who decide, do and advise when an agency sets a Zero Trust target, assesses against it and acts on what it finds: the CIO and executive sponsor, the Zero Trust program lead, the CISO and ISSO, the authorizing official, the five pillar owners and the owner of the naming and identity plane, security operations, the assessment operators, control assessors, and the privacy and records offices. A responsibility matrix covers each decision, a table maps each pillar to its owner and its book, and a role map shows the access each team needs in the assessment tools.

Zero Trust programs don’t usually fail for lack of owners. They fail because every owner is a pillar owner. Identity engineering owns Conditional Access, endpoint engineering owns Intune, network engineering owns the edge, application owners own their applications, and the data office owns classification. Each runs its own tool, reports on its own schedule, and can truthfully say its pillar is improving. Nobody owns the question this book keeps asking: what is the agency’s posture, across all five pillars and every estate it runs, and what does the number on the cover page actually measure?

Chapter 01 argues that Zero Trust maturity is a property of the whole agency and that three assessments stapled together are not one assessment. Chapter 02 names Governance as the cross-cutting capability programs invest in least. This chapter turns both arguments into names. CISA’s Zero Trust Maturity Model makes the same point about people: adoption needs engagement from senior leadership, IT staff, data and system owners and users, and it requires agencies to move stove-piped IT services and staff into a coordinated strategy with agency-wide buy-in for a common architecture and governance policies.1 OMB M-22-09 says agency leadership and the whole “C-suite” should be aligned behind the overhaul, and it required each agency to designate a zero trust strategy implementation lead within 30 days.2 What neither document says is who decides when a ScubaGear omission expires, or who can declare that a Zero Trust Assessment score covers the agency. That is the program’s recommended model, set out below as design.

Book 01, Chapter 09 sets out the program-wide model and the Risk Management Framework roles. Book 04, Chapter 06, Book 05, Chapter 06 and Book 09, Chapter 09 apply it to access, endpoints and the network. This chapter applies it to the posture that sits across all of them. Adapt the roles to the agency’s governance, but keep one decider per row.

Why the posture needs its own owner

Three facts from earlier chapters make the posture a separate thing to own.

The first is scope. CISA’s ScubaGear and Microsoft’s Zero Trust Assessment overlap mainly on Microsoft Entra ID and, as of version 3.1.0, on Defender threat policies (Chapter 05), and both stop at the edge of Microsoft’s platform. Every other cloud provider offers its own native posture tooling, and the on-premises virtualization estate has none of theirs (Chapter 06). A provider assessing its own platform is scoped to that platform. It has no view of the other providers, the network layer or the hypervisors, and no reason to reconcile against them. The agency is the only party that can hold one posture across all of them, so the agency, not any provider’s tool or account team, has to own it. The governance principles paper puts this as an ask: assign agency-level Zero Trust ownership separately from any cloud service provider relationship.

The second is correlation. A finding about a stale service-principal credential matters to the identity pillar, the applications pillar and whichever data the principal can reach. If those three pillars resolve objects through three naming systems, someone has to correlate the finding by hand, and in most programs nobody is assigned to. The governance principles paper asks agencies to name a cross-pillar correlation owner. In the program’s model that is the Zero Trust program lead.

The third is risk. CISA’s maturity model is explicit that it does not address risk acceptance, among other things,3 and it describes itself as a general guide, not a strict set of requirements.4 Risk acceptance sits where it always has, with the authorizing official under the Risk Management Framework. The authorizing official is the only organizational official who can accept security and privacy risk on the organization’s behalf, approves plans of action and milestones, and decides whether a significant change needs reauthorization.5 A maturity target, a tool score and a risk decision are three different things owned by three different people. Programs that blur them end up with a tool vendor’s dashboard quietly standing in for an authorization decision.

Decision rights and work are different things

The program separates three kinds of involvement, as Book 01 does. A role decides when it is accountable for the outcome and can say no. A role does the work when it builds, configures, runs or remediates. A role is consulted when the decision can’t be made well without its input, even though the decision isn’t its to make.

The split matters for Zero Trust because many assessment decisions are risk decisions that look like configuration. Marking a ScubaGear policy as omitted removes it from the report. Marking a result incorrect changes what the tool says about the tenant. Treating a skipped Zero Trust Assessment check as a pass, because the license for it isn’t in the agency’s boundary, overstates the posture. ScubaGear’s own documentation warns that omitting policies or marking results incorrect can introduce blind spots, and says omissions must only be made if approved within the organization’s security risk management process.6 CISA’s BOD 25-01 says the same thing at the directive level: authorizing officials may accept risk for deviations from the mandatory SCuBA policies to meet operational needs, under agency policy, and agencies must identify and explain those deviations when they report.7 The decision behind an edit to a configuration file therefore belongs to the authorizing official, even though an engineer types it.

The roles

The CIO and executive sponsor set the Zero Trust strategy, its scope and its funding, and they break ties. Under the Risk Management Framework the CIO designates the senior agency information security officer, maintains security policy and reports to the head of the agency on the security program’s effectiveness, and, with authorizing officials, allocates the resources that protect the agency’s systems.8 For this book that means three decisions. The scope decision: which estates the whole-agency score covers (every tenant, every cloud, the network, the virtualization estate), stated in writing so the score can’t quietly shrink to the estates that assess well. The sequence decision: Chapter 08 closes BOD 25-01 findings before the maturity assessment, and that order competes for the same engineers as every other deadline. And the tie-break: identity engineering, endpoint engineering and network engineering can each reasonably claim a cross-pillar control, such as device posture in a token, and the CIO decides which team owns it.

The Zero Trust program lead owns the posture. Many agencies will give this role to the zero trust strategy implementation lead M-22-09 required them to designate.9 In the program’s model the lead owns four things. The definition of the whole-agency score: what it covers, what it excludes, and the unified record each assessed object resolves to; a score that can’t point to that record is reported as partial assessments, not one. The cross-pillar correlation: when one finding touches several pillars, the lead’s team routes it and makes sure it isn’t closed in one pillar while open in another. The assessment calendar: which tool runs against which estate, when, with which pinned version, and when results are re-run after remediation. And the prioritized program in Chapter 10: turning findings into a ranked worklist that each pillar owner executes. The lead decides priority and sequence. The lead doesn’t accept risk; that goes to the authorizing official. The lead’s independence from any cloud provider relationship is the point of the role, so the lead shouldn’t also be the agency’s account owner for its largest cloud vendor.

The CISO and ISSO decide what the posture has to meet. The CISO, as the senior agency information security officer, carries out the CIO’s security responsibilities and is the CIO’s primary liaison to authorizing officials, system owners, common control providers and system security officers.10 In the program’s model the CISO sets the control requirements for each pillar, the evidence standard (what makes an assessment output acceptable as evidence: a pinned tool version, a recorded run time, an unaltered output file, a hash), which assessment tools the agency may run against production tenants, and the rules for the accounts that run them. The CISO also owns the requirement that assessment outputs are handled as sensitive data, because Microsoft warns that the Zero Trust Assessment report and its export folder contain sensitive tenant information that threat actors could use, and should be shared only with authorized personnel.11 A ScubaGear report describes the same weaknesses from a different angle and gets the same handling. The ISSO for each system assembles the evidence, keeps the system’s plan of action and milestones current and reviews the security impact of each remediation change, as the system security officer role does under the RMF.12

The authorizing official accepts residual risk. For this book that covers four kinds of decision. Deviations from a mandatory SCuBA policy, which BOD 25-01 lets authorizing officials accept under agency policy.13 Omissions and incorrect-result markings in the ScubaGear configuration file that have no expiration date, since a standing omission is a standing risk acceptance. The plan of action and milestones for each system, which the authorizing official approves.14 And the degree of assessor independence, which the authorizing official determines explicitly when an assessment supports an authorization or ongoing authorization.15 Most agencies have several authorizing officials, so the whole-agency posture spans more than one of them. The program lead aggregates; each authorizing official accepts risk only for the systems and common controls they authorize. The authorizing official can delegate day-to-day work to a designated representative, but not the acceptance of risk itself.16

The pillar owners do the remediation and own their pillar’s maturity. The program maps each pillar of CISA’s model, which M-22-09 also uses to organize its goals,17 to the team that owns it after modernization, and each team’s book assigns it decisions in detail (see the table below). Pillar owners triage findings in their pillar, remediate through their normal change control, and record the closure evidence that lets the finding be re-tested. Many of them are also common control providers in RMF terms: the identity platform, for example, provides controls that every application inherits. A common control provider is responsible for implementing, assessing and monitoring those controls and for producing plans of action and milestones for any that are deficient.18 A pillar owner decides how to fix a finding. A pillar owner doesn’t decide that a finding doesn’t need fixing.

The owner of the naming and identity plane holds the unified record the score depends on. Chapter 06 explains why Network, every cloud and the virtualization estate have to resolve objects against one authoritative naming and identity plane before their assessments mean the same thing, and why that plane, once it exists, is the most valuable target in the estate: a complete asset map that may also carry reconciliation privileges across every domain. In the program’s model identity engineering owns the identity half and the DNS/DDI team (Book 08) owns naming and addressing. Whoever owns it, the plane is governed as a Tier-0 asset: administrative access is eligible rather than standing and time-boxed, it has its own monitored telemetry stream, and its design assumes it will be attacked directly.

Security operations receives what the posture emits. OMB M-26-14, which rescinded M-21-31, requires that logs be readily available to the agency’s top-level security operations center for continuous event monitoring and for threat hunting, investigation, response and forensics, and that the logs collected identify the identity used for each operation and every change to privilege levels.19 For this book that means three duties. Security operations consumes drift findings (a control that passed last interval and fails this one), because some of them are the first sign of an intrusion rather than an administrative slip. It monitors the assessment accounts themselves, which hold broad read access to every tenant they assess. And it is consulted on every omission and every exception, because it is the team that finds out when one is used.

The assessment operators run the tools. In most agencies this is a security engineering or compliance team inside the CISO’s organization. They run ScubaGear and the Zero Trust Assessment on the calendar the program lead sets, under accounts the CISO approves, on hosts inside the authorization boundary, and they deliver unaltered outputs to the evidence repository. BOD 25-01 required agencies to deploy the SCuBA assessment tools for in-scope tenants and report results to CISA, either continuously through CISA’s monitoring integration or quarterly in a CISA-approved machine-readable format.20 CISA’s implementation guidance offers four routes: CISA-hosted ScubaConnect, self-hosted ScubaConnect, an agency-hosted integration through CLAW or TALON, and manual quarterly upload through CyberScope (Chapter 04).21 Whichever route the agency picks, the operators own it. They don’t edit results, and they don’t decide omissions.

Control assessors assess with the independence the authorizing official sets. Under the RMF the assessor determines whether controls are implemented correctly, operating as intended and producing the desired outcome, reports deficiencies and their severity, and can recommend corrective actions.22 SP 800-53 provides for independent assessors both for point-in-time assessments and for ongoing monitoring.23 In the program’s model a ScubaGear or Zero Trust Assessment run is evidence the assessor reads, not an assessment the tool performs. The assessor can re-run the evidence assembly, compare hashes and walk a finding back to its raw output (Chapter 09), and that is what makes the tool output assessable rather than merely archived.

The privacy and records offices are consulted. The Zero Trust Assessment’s own guide warns that reports, exports and diagnostics can contain tenant configuration and personal information.24 The senior agency official for privacy is consulted before assessment outputs are retained, shared outside the security organization or loaded into a reporting tool. The records officer sets how long each output is kept. That retention is a records decision, not a tool default.

Six rows need comment.

The score row gives the program lead the decision because the definition of “whole agency” is the thing every other role has an incentive to narrow. A pillar owner whose estate assesses badly would prefer it excluded; a cloud account team would prefer its own tool’s score to stand in for the whole. The lead decides, the authorizing official and the assessors are consulted, and the exclusions are written down.

The identity-plane row gives that decision to the plane’s owner rather than the program lead, because the plane is infrastructure with its own privileged access and blast radius, not a reporting artifact. The program lead depends on it and is consulted.

The omission rows split on time, as the exception rows do in Books 05, 08 and 09. The CISO can approve an omission that expires, because it is a bounded, reviewable decision recorded in the configuration file itself. An omission, deviation or incorrect-result marking with no end date is a standing risk acceptance and goes to the authorizing official, which matches BOD 25-01’s assignment of deviation risk to authorizing officials.25

The priority row gives the program lead priority and sequence, while the remediation row gives each pillar owner the decision about how to fix. The lead can say a finding is first; only the pillar owner can say how it is closed, and the closure evidence has to satisfy the CISO’s standard.

The drift row gives the authorizing official the decision because drift is change to an authorized system. A control that passed every interval and failed the last one is a different finding from one that has always failed, and the authorizing official decides whether it amounts to a significant change.26 The program lead and security operations prepare it; the ScubaGear integration paper’s adoption step is to treat these findings as artifacts the authorizing official sees, not only security operations.

The native-tool row is the governance principle in one line. A cloud provider’s native posture tool is admitted as evidence for its own platform, by the program lead, under the CISO’s evidence standard. It is never admitted as the agency’s score.

Three notes. The Zero Trust Assessment’s guide is explicit that a highly privileged administrator should be used for the initial consent only, and that consent doesn’t assign runtime roles: the assessment account should be a reader.31 Microsoft’s get-started page still describes a Global Administrator granting first-run consent and later runs as Global Reader;32 the program follows the narrower guide in the tool’s own repository. The assessment accounts are among the most sensitive identities in the design, because between them they can read the configuration of every tenant the agency runs; they are eligible rather than standing where the platform allows, and their sign-ins are logged to the top-level SOC as M-26-14 requires for privileged operations.33 And the Zero Trust Assessment sends a telemetry event by default that carries the tenant ID; it can be disabled with -DisableTelemetry.34 Whether to disable it is a CISO decision recorded with the tool approval, not an operator’s choice on the day.
