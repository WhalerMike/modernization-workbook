# Chapter 13 — Records, Evidence and Retention

*The program calls three different things a record, and federal law has a fourth meaning that overrides the other three: whatever the scripts write as evidence, how long it lives and when it dies is a records decision, not an engineering default.*

::: {.callout-note}
## In this chapter
The four things the workbook means by "record": the authoritative technical record, audit evidence, a federal record and operational data, and how they overlap. When audit evidence and logs are federal records, and the General Records Schedule items that commonly apply to them, checked against the National Archives on November 2, 2026. Disposition authority; legal holds and records freezes, with the steps to suspend an evidence purge or rotation; how immutable (write once, read many) storage interacts with disposition; transfer when a system is decommissioned; and who owns which decision. It closes with a likely records category for each kit's evidence output, as design guidance to confirm with the agency records officer.
:::

The book has used the word "record" in an architectural sense from the start. [Chapter 03](03-one-authoritative-record.md) makes the authoritative record the program's source of truth. [Chapter 07](07-evidence-over-attestation.md) makes the evidence record, produced by systems and chained by hash, the basis for an authorization. The kits now write those evidence records: the Ticket-to-Machine kit's evidence export, the deployment scripts' step records, and the drift pipeline's evidence packages. None of them decides how long its output is kept, who may destroy it, or what happens to it when a lawyer says "keep everything." Federal records law decides that, and it uses the word "record" in a sense of its own.

This chapter separates the meanings, says where federal records law reaches the program's evidence, and gives each kit's output a likely place in the General Records Schedules. [Chapter 09](09-who-governs.md#who-keeps-the-record-as-a-record) already names the records officer as the owner of the authoritative record's retention. [Book 00, Chapter 10](../b00-orientation/10-retirement-steady-state-and-success.md#records-before-hardware-the-decommissioning-checklist) gives the decommissioning checklist for the directory-era systems, and [Book 12, Chapter 10](../b12-security-operations/10-who-runs-security-operations.md) covers security log retention. This chapter doesn't repeat them; it covers the evidence the program itself produces.

## Four things called a record

| | Authoritative technical record | Audit evidence | Federal record | Operational data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The governed repository, which holds the authority for each fact and the desired state; the system that owns a fact, such as the HR system of record for employment, holds the fact itself | Proof that a control operated: the kits' hash-chained JSON evidence records, drift reports, manifests, step outputs | A legal status: recorded information an agency makes or receives and preserves, or should preserve, as evidence of its activities or for its informational value[^c13-3301] | The working content of a running system: directory objects, workflow state, queue messages, caches, telemetry in flight |
| **Defined by** | Program design ([Chapter 03](03-one-authoritative-record.md)) | Program design and the controls it supports ([Chapter 07](07-evidence-over-attestation.md)) | 44 U.S.C. §3301 and 36 CFR 1220.18 and 1222.10[^c13-3301][^c13-1220][^c13-1222] | The system's own design |
| **The question it answers** | Which copy is right? | Did the control work, and can anyone re-check it? | Must the agency keep it, for how long, and may it be destroyed? | What is the system doing now? |
| **Who decides how long it lives** | The records officer, inside the records schedule; the steward implements it | The records officer, through the schedule; the ISSO says what the authorization needs | The Archivist approves the schedule; the agency applies it[^c13-3303a][^c13-1226] | The system owner, inside the records schedule that covers the system |

The columns aren't exclusive. "Federal record" isn't a kind of data; it is a status that the other three can have. The authoritative record's commit history documents who changed what and who approved it, and evidence bundles document how the agency operated its systems, so both are made and preserved "as evidence of the organization, functions, policies, decisions, procedures, operations, or other activities" of the government, in the statute's words.[^c13-3301] Operational data can be a federal record too, but it is usually governed by the schedule for the system that holds it: the technology schedules, GRS 3.1 and 3.2, each say they don't apply to "system data or content."[^c13-grs31][^c13-grs32]

Three consequences follow (design guidance):

- **Treat audit evidence as a federal record by default.** It is made to be preserved as evidence of operations, which is the statutory test. NARA's regulations say that if a clear determination can't be made, materials should be treated as records.[^c13-1222]
- **A copy can be a record.** Whether a document is a record "does not depend upon whether it contains unique information," and duplicates can each have record status depending on how they are used.[^c13-1222] The copy of an evidence package that an ISSO reviews for an authorization decision is not automatically a convenience copy.
- **Unscheduled means permanent.** Federal records whose disposition NARA hasn't approved "must be treated as permanent until a final disposition is approved."[^c13-1220] A new kind of evidence with no schedule item can't be deleted on a rotation the engineers chose.

Only two exclusions in the statute matter here: library and reference material, and "duplicate copies of records preserved only for convenience."[^c13-3301] NARA calls these nonrecord materials, requires agencies to keep them identifiable and segregable from records, and lets them be purged without NARA approval when no longer needed.[^c13-1222] A developer's local `out/` folder, after the package it produced is in the evidence store, is the usual example.

## When audit evidence and logs are federal records

Audit evidence and security logs are almost always federal records. The narrower question is which records schedule item covers them, because the item sets the retention period. The Archivist issues the General Records Schedules for records common to several or all agencies, and their disposal authorizations are mandatory.[^c13-3303a] An agency can also have its own NARA-approved schedule for records the GRS doesn't fit.

Two cautions apply before the table. First, the security minimums don't replace the schedule. OMB M-26-14 sets log retention minimums (searchable for 6 months, retrievable for a year) and says that meeting them "does not relieve agencies of the obligation to comply with other applicable requirements, such as those established by agency-specific or government-wide records schedules."[^c13-m2614] Second, the cybersecurity logging items are being revised. GRS 3.2 items 035 and 036 still cite the rescinded M-21-31, and NARA's status page lists their revision, for M-21-31's rescission and M-26-14's issuance, as "Drafting" in Transmittal 38, as of October 2026.[^c13-grs-status] Until it is issued, how those two items apply is the records officer's call.

Notes that change how the table is read:

- **The schedules come from different transmittals.** GRS 3.1 is from Transmittal 30 (November 2019), GRS 3.2 from Transmittal 33 (January 2023), GRS 4.1 and 5.2 from Transmittal 34 (June 2023) and GRS 5.1 from Transmittal 28 (July 2017). Cite the item and its authority, not the transmittal, in the agency's directive.
- **Significant incidents need their own schedule.** GRS 3.2 item 020 notes that significant incidents "must be documented in program records," scheduled separately with an SF 115.[^c13-grs32] Evidence pulled into an incident investigation may move with it.
- **The transitory and intermediary items exclude evidence of decision-making.** Both GRS 5.2 items apply only to records not required "to initiate, sustain, evaluate, or provide evidence of decision-making," and item 010 excludes data extracts that significantly change the source content.[^c13-grs52] A drift report that a go/no-go decision rested on isn't transitory, however short its life in a pipeline.
- **"Longer retention is authorized if required for business use"** appears in most items. It allows an agency to keep evidence longer for an authorization cycle or an audit; it doesn't allow destruction earlier.

## Disposition authority

Every federal record, "including those created or maintained for the Government by a contractor," must be covered by a NARA-approved agency schedule or the General Records Schedules, and applying an approved schedule is mandatory.[^c13-1225][^c13-1226] Disposition means the actions taken on records no longer needed for current business; a disposition authority is the legal authorization for keeping and disposing of them.[^c13-1220] Three rules matter for evidence:

- **The agency's directive names the item.** Agencies must issue disposition authorities through their internal directives within six months of approval, and the directive "must cite the legal authority (GRS or SF 115 and item numbers) for each schedule item."[^c13-1226] An record class the program creates should appear in that directive, or in the records officer's file plan under it, before its first purge.
- **Early disposal is unlawful destruction.** NARA defines unlawful or accidental destruction to include disposal of an unscheduled or permanent record, disposal of a temporary record before the end of its approved retention period, and "disposal of a record subject to a FOIA request, litigation hold, or any other hold requirement."[^c13-1230] Agency heads must report it to NARA, and the statute requires them to notify the Archivist of any actual, impending or threatened unlawful "corruption, deletion, erasure, or other destruction."[^c13-1230][^c13-3106]
- **An agency schedule and a GRS item can both reach the same records.** The statute says that, as between the two, "application of the authorization providing for the shorter retention period shall be required," subject to 44 U.S.C. §2909, under which NARA can also withdraw disposal authority.[^c13-3303a][^c13-1226] The records officer resolves that; engineers shouldn't pick the longer period on their own.

In the program's design, each record class has a row in a retention register kept in the governed repository: the class, the kits and paths that write it, the GRS or agency item the records officer assigned, the disposition instruction in words, the event that starts the clock, and where the recordkeeping copy lives (design guidance). Purge jobs read the register; they don't carry their own numbers. That is the same rule [Book 12, Chapter 12](../b12-security-operations/12-evidence-metrics-and-continuous-monitoring.md#retention-tiers-and-tamper-protected-copies) applies to log tables, and it keeps a retention change a governed edit ([Chapter 04](04-change-control-and-provenance.md)) rather than a script change.

## Legal holds and records freezes

A hold suspends disposition. NARA's regulations put it in two places. The definition of unlawful destruction covers any record "subject to a FOIA request, litigation hold, or any other hold requirement to retain the records," and an electronic recordkeeping system must be able to "apply records hold or freeze on disposition when required."[^c13-1230][^c13-1236] Beyond a hold for a specific matter:

- **Temporary extension.** An agency may keep records past their approved retention period when "special circumstances alter the normal administrative, legal, or fiscal value of the records." It needs NARA's written approval unless the records will be needed for less than a year, a court order requires retention, or a schedule change has been requested. Once the circumstances have passed, the agency "must destroy the records in accordance with the NARA-approved disposition instructions."[^c13-1226]
- **Privacy limits.** An extension must stay consistent with the Privacy Act's requirement that records about individuals be kept only if relevant and necessary, and records whose disposal a statute requires can't be kept unless a court orders it.[^c13-1226]
- **A freeze from NARA.** NARA can withdraw disposal authorizations "to ensure the preservation of Government records," for one agency's schedule items or for a type of record across agencies, and notifies agencies in writing.[^c13-1226]

Who issues a hold is an agency matter: counsel for litigation, the FOIA office for a request, the records officer for a NARA freeze or extension. The program's part is to make sure a hold actually stops every deletion path for the evidence it covers.

### How to suspend an evidence purge or rotation

The kits' packaging scripts don't delete evidence; the drift pipeline refuses to overwrite a package that already exists. Deletion happens around them, in storage retention settings, pipeline artifact expiry, platform table retention and clean-up jobs. The steps below are design guidance; the hold itself, and its scope, come from counsel or the records officer.

1. **Record the hold in the governed repository.** The steward writes a hold record through the normal change path: an identifier, the issuing office, the date, the scope (systems, record classes, date range, tenants), and no end date. The hold record points at the retention register rows it suspends. Like an exception in [Chapter 09](09-who-governs.md#who-accepts-exceptions), it never edits the register; it sits beside it.
2. **List every deletion path for the evidence in scope.** For the kits that means at least: the immutable evidence container's retention interval and any lifecycle or clean-up rule on it; the pipeline's short-lived hand-off artifacts (the drift pipeline keeps its drift report as a workflow artifact for 7 days); the issue tracker or finding table if it archives or purges closed items; table retention in the SIEM or log workspace that ingests the same evidence; and working folders on runners and workstations.
3. **Suspend each path, closest to the data first.** Turn off the lifecycle or clean-up rule. Extend retention, or apply the storage service's legal-hold setting if it has one, so items whose interval ends during the hold can't be deleted. Copy short-lived artifacts in scope into the held store, with their SHA-256 recorded at the copy, before they expire. Raise table retention where the hold reaches logs.
4. **Make purge jobs fail closed.** Every job that deletes evidence reads the hold records first and refuses to delete anything in scope. If it can't read them, it deletes nothing and raises a finding.
5. **Keep evidence of the suspension.** Export the retention and lifecycle settings before and after, with times and the identity that changed them, and add the export to the evidence store. The hold record, the exports and the release are themselves records; GRS 4.1 item 020 covers records about "transferring, destroying, and retrieving records," though counsel's own hold files are usually scheduled with the matter.[^c13-grs41]
6. **Release in writing, then resume disposition.** When the issuing office releases the hold, the steward closes the hold record, restores the settings, and lets disposition run under the register. Records past their date are destroyed as the schedule instructs, the rule 36 CFR 1226.18(c) sets for the end of a temporary extension.[^c13-1226]

## Immutable storage and disposition

The program writes evidence once and keeps it in storage that can't be changed or deleted until a retention interval ends: in the drift pipeline's design, a container with a locked time-based retention policy ([Book 19, Chapter 09](../b19-developer-workbook/09-drift-pipelines-and-evidence.md#the-evidence-package) covers the platform settings). That serves records law well. NARA requires controls for electronic records that protect authenticity "against unauthorized addition, deletion, alteration, use, and concealment," and integrity through audit trails that keep records "complete and unaltered."[^c13-1236] Write-once storage and the hash chain from [Chapter 07](07-evidence-over-attestation.md) provide both. It also interacts with disposition in five ways the records officer should hear about before the lock is set (design guidance):

- **A lock longer than the schedule blocks lawful destruction.** Most items in the table allow longer retention for business use, so over-retention is usually permitted. It isn't when a statute requires disposal or when the Privacy Act's relevance test applies, and after a temporary extension ends the agency "must destroy" the records.[^c13-1226] [Book 19, Chapter 09](../b19-developer-workbook/09-drift-pipelines-and-evidence.md#verified-facts) notes that the drift pipeline's locked policy can't be shortened, so set the lock to the shortest period the schedule certainly requires, and extend it when needed.
- **A lock shorter than the schedule ends protection early.** When the interval ends, the blob becomes deletable. Disposition still runs from the register, not from the lock's expiry; a clean-up rule keyed to the lock would destroy temporary records early, which is unlawful destruction.[^c13-1230]
- **A retention lock isn't a hold.** It runs out on its own date whatever is pending. Holds are applied separately, as in the steps above.
- **Storage isn't a recordkeeping system on its own.** NARA lists the functions electronic recordkeeping needs: declare, capture, organize (associate each record "with an approved records schedule and disposition instruction"), maintain security, manage access, preserve, and execute disposition, including holds.[^c13-1236] Immutable storage supplies one of them. The manifest, the retention register and the hold records supply most of the rest; the records officer decides whether the combination meets the agency's recordkeeping requirements.
- **Backups don't count.** "System and file backup processes and media do not provide the appropriate recordkeeping functionalities and must not be used as the agency electronic recordkeeping system."[^c13-1236] A snapshot of the evidence store is a backup under GRS 3.2 items 040 and 041, not a second recordkeeping copy.[^c13-grs32]

## Transfer at decommissioning

Evidence often outlives the system that produced it. A configuration and change record under GRS 3.1 item 030 runs five years from the day the system is superseded or terminated, and a special-accountability access record under GRS 3.2 item 031 runs six years from the account's termination.[^c13-grs31][^c13-grs32] [Book 00, Chapter 10](../b00-orientation/10-retirement-steady-state-and-success.md#records-before-hardware-the-decommissioning-checklist) gives the checklist for retiring the directory-era systems. For the program's own evidence, the same rules add four points:

- **Plan the migration before the retirement.** Agencies must determine whether the approved retention period is longer than the life of the system holding the records and, "If so, plan for the migration of the records to a new system before the current system is retired," keeping the link between records and their metadata, and capturing metadata "for both the records and the migration process."[^c13-1236] For evidence, the metadata includes the manifests, the chain heads and the retention register rows.
- **Keep the means to verify.** A hash-chained package is only useful if someone can still recompute the chain. Records must be "located, retrieved, presented, and interpreted" for their whole retention period,[^c13-1236] so the verify commands (`evidence_export.py --verify-dir`, `drift_pipeline.py verify`, `step_evidence.py --verify`), or a documented equivalent such as `sha256sum -c`, are kept with the evidence store (design guidance).
- **Transfer permanent records to NARA.** Permanent records are transferred when the schedule says or after 30 years, in electronic form with appropriate metadata since June 30, 2024, and with the documentation NARA's transfer rules require.[^c13-1235][^c13-m2307] None of the GRS items above is permanent; if the records officer finds evidence that is, or an agency schedule makes it so, it goes to NARA rather than to destruction.
- **File the disposition record.** What was migrated, transferred or destroyed, under which item and on whose approval, goes to the records officer and into the G4 or G5 evidence packet, as step 9 of Book 00's checklist says.

## Who owns what

[Chapter 09](09-who-governs.md#who-keeps-the-record-as-a-record) assigns the authoritative record's retention to the records officer, and [Book 12, Chapter 10](../b12-security-operations/10-who-runs-security-operations.md) does the same for security logs. The program applies the same split to its evidence. The records officer's authority comes from regulation: agencies must assign records management responsibility "to a person and office with appropriate authority," assign records responsibilities in each program area, and build records requirements into the design of electronic systems.[^c13-1220] The other rows are design guidance.

| Role | Owns | Decides | Doesn't decide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Records officer | The retention register's legal column; the agency's directive and file plan | Which GRS or agency item covers each record class; when a class needs a new schedule; temporary extensions and NARA requests; whether evidence is permanent; destruction approvals | What an authorization package must contain |
| ISSO | What evidence the system's authorization and continuous monitoring need | The minimum the security documentation needs kept, for the authorizing official; whether a package is complete and verifies; when to escalate a gap | The retention period, which can be longer than security needs but not shorter than the schedule |
| System owner | The system that produces the evidence and its storage | How the system implements the register: storage, locks, lifecycle rules, purge jobs that read the register and the holds | Which schedule item applies, or whether to delete early |
| Steward | The governed repository: the register, hold records, change history | That every retention or hold change goes through the change path ([Chapter 04](04-change-control-and-provenance.md)) | Retention values |
| Counsel and the FOIA office | Holds for litigation and requests | When a hold starts, its scope and when it is released | How the hold is carried out in each system |

The handoffs are where evidence is usually lost: a storage engineer shortens a retention interval to save cost, or an expiry rule nobody listed keeps deleting during a hold. Both are prevented by the same rule: retention numbers and holds live in the governed repository, and systems read them.
